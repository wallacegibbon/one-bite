## 1. Art direction

**Voxel, but not Minecraft.** References: the block language of *Minecraft* + the saturated palette of *Trove* + Ghibli's "plants have intentions".

Three hard rules:

1. **Every plant has something it "wants to do"**: fruit self-glows (wants to be seen), vines grow down (looking for water), mushrooms ring up (claiming ground).
2. **Buildings are grown, not laid**: Lanternrow's houses are timber frames with mossy-soil infill; Starfall Keep is brick plus lumenstone lamps; the Old Orchard is a ring of broken wall around the oldest tree in the valley.
3. **There are only three light sources**: sun/moon, lumenstone and embers (self-lit blocks), fruit (a fruit block lights itself, and so does one you carry), and one animal (the lamp jellyfish parts are built emissive — but **no body you can wear glows**). **There is no ambient fill light** — at night, without a Glowcap, you genuinely cannot see.

### Face lighting (baked, not real-time)

Each face gets a fixed brightness by normal, then vertex AO is multiplied in:

| Face | Brightness |
|---|---|
| +Y (top) | 1.00 |
| +Z | 0.88 |
| +X | 0.78 |
| −X | 0.66 |
| −Z | 0.58 |
| −Y (bottom) | 0.42 |

AO has four steps: `[1.00, 0.78, 0.60, 0.46]`, computed from each vertex's three neighbours (edge, edge, corner).

That table is for blocks with hard edges. A block may declare `soft: 1` instead, and then it keeps the same ordering of faces but never drops below **0.74** and takes no AO at all (`SOFT_S = 0.55 + 0.45·s`). Cloud (16) is the one that needed it: cloud is a block, so it went through the face table and the ambient occlusion of the cloud deck around it, and a bottom face in the shade of the block above measured 0.42 × 0.46 = 0.19 of its own colour. Cloud is 94% white, so the result was a dark grey lump floating in a bright sky — a black block in the sky, which is what it looked like. Measured in the frame: a cloud now averages **113** against the sky's 92; with the flag removed it averaged **70**, darker than the sky behind it. The Grey Veil wall is built out of the same block, so the veil reads as pale mist columns rather than as stone.

A canopy takes the same flag, for the same reason. A crown is a blob of leaf blocks; its underside is the face you look at when you stand under the tree, and the ring of neighbours the crevice test samples under it **is** the crown -- the dark ring under a tree is the tree. So the five canopy blocks (leaves 7, needles 8, silver leaves 9, dark leaves 27, willow leaves 28) are `soft: 1` too.

That still left the crevice test itself, which asks whether a face sits in a crevice and had been read as a brightness rule. A face looking out at open sky is by definition not in one, yet the underside of a crown scored the darkest step, because the solid all around it is the same crown. So every face is now also asked how far it looks along its own normal before it hits anything: `OPEN_R = 4` cells of clear air and it takes no crevice darkening at all; anything solid within two cells and it keeps all of it -- that is the contact shadow a cliff, a roof rim and a cave lip need; in between, the amount is interpolated. A bottom face also lifts toward the soft bottom shade, because the reason the table makes a bottom face 0.42 -- nobody looks at one -- is not true of a face hanging in open air.

Measured over the whole map, counting only the faces that have eight cells of clear air along their normal -- the faces a player sees against the sky, 107,880 of them: **60,506** rendered under 60/255, 56% of them, mean 73, worst 10. The exposure term alone takes that to 19,277 (18%); with the canopy flag as well, **15,421 (14%)**, mean 93, worst 39. What is left is the face table doing what it is told: a −Z face of a dark block is dark, and that is the fake sun, not a hole. Widening the ray to the widest of five rays was measured and changed nothing, so the ray stays straight. The extra ray costs nothing measurable: a full re-mesh of 100 chunks is ~100 ms before and after. In the rendered frame, over 216 views across the map, pixels darker than 55 with sky brighter than 130 within five pixels of them fell from **1.46%** of the frame to **0.68%**. The same measure at the two low-sun hours (07:00, 17:00) is **0.53%**, so noon is the worst case rather than the luckiest one. `game/test/play.js` reads the frame at the two places a player actually stands under this -- under a conifer crown, and under the town roof over the lantern road: a hanging face may be darker than the sky, but a quarter of its pixels must reach **45**/255 and the darkest twentieth must be past **35**. With the shading reverted those read 31 and 24 for the crown, 22 and 19 for the roof.

### Creature models

Assembled from voxel parts: one mesh + one pivot per part. A part may name a `parent`, so a limb has a joint -- shoulder to elbow, hip to knee. Parts are authored in the bind pose, and for the owl the bind pose is the folded wing: a perched owl is the shape you look at most of the day.

**Every model faces +Z**, and a heading of `yaw` in this game means the direction `(sin yaw, -cos yaw)`: yaw 0 is north (-Z) and yaw grows toward +X. The rotation that carries +Z onto a heading is therefore `PI - yaw`, and it exists in exactly one place, `M4.faceY`. Writing `yaw + PI` -- which all three call sites used to -- is exact only when heading due north or due south, and mirrors the body about the Z axis everywhere else: seen from behind, a body walking east showed its face, in first person the hand pointed sideways, and every animal in the valley walked backwards whenever it headed east or west. The `camera` step's angle checks cannot see it, because a mirrored model has the same angles, so `game/test/play.js` reads the frame: it stands the body at each of the four cardinal headings with the lens behind it, projects the head to the screen, and demands the back of the head -- hair, not a face -- at every one of them.

| Animation | Used for | Formula |
|---|---|---|
| `flap` | upper wing | `rz = −sign·beat·(0.22+0.78·air)`, `beat = sin(t·11)·flap`, the downstroke full and the recovery 0.42 of it. A symmetric sine is a windmill; a hard downstroke and a soft recovery is a wing. `flap` is how hard the body is beating — see the wing beat below |
| `fold` | forewing, parented to the wing | folds on the upstroke only: `rz = sign·(beat<0 ? −beat·0.5 : 0)·air` |
| `walk` | thigh / upper arm | `rx = −asin(sin(0.9·stride·amp)·footAt(u))` — the angle that puts the foot where it belongs along its own stride. `u = gait + (sign < 0 ? 0.5 : 0)` is where the body is in its own stride, one whole cycle per stride of ground, the two sides of a body half a stride apart, and the caller advances it by the ground the body covered and never by a clock. `stride` is the part's own share of the swing: a frog's legs take none of it, because a frog does not walk on land, it hops |
| `knee` | shin | a knee folds one way only, and only while the foot is off the ground: `rx = bend·0.62·stride·fold(u)·move`, where `fold(u)` is nothing at all through the stance and one hump through the swing. When it folds is said by the stride itself and not by a quarter cycle of a clock: a model with no ankle has nowhere for a folded planted knee to go but down. `bend` says which way this model's knee folds (absent = the human's way) and the `sign` mirror is deliberately **not** applied to the fold |
| `elbow` | forearm | the same fold on an arm, smaller, with a little of it always held, **and the arm held the more bent the faster the body goes**: `rx = bend·(0.12+0.3·fold(u)·move+0.55·move²)` — an arm hanging dead straight is a stick, and a straight arm swung through 2.3 strides a second is a pendulum the body has to stop and start again twice a stride. A body standing still holds **0.12 rad** as it always did, a body walking still lets its arm hang (**0.14**), and a body running carries it bent (**0.61** held all the way through the stride, **0.89** at the widest moment of the swing). An arm swings opposite the leg on the same side, which is what the half stride `sign` buys it |
| `twist` | torso | `ry = footAt(u)·move·0.16` — the part above the legs turning against them, on the stride and not on the clock, or the shoulders and the feet are two unrelated things at any speed other than the one the clock happens to agree with |
| `nod` | head | `rx = sin(t·1.9)·(move·0.06+0.02)` |
| `wag` | tail | `ry = sin(t·1.6)·(move·0.5+0.12)` |
| `bob` | body | in the air it **is** the wing beat: `dy = −max(0,beat)·0.16`, the body drops as the wings push down. Two unrelated sines is what made a flying body look like a body with two loose parts bolted to it. The heave goes with the stroke rather than the clock, so a body riding its wings down drops 0.024 m instead of 0.16 m. On foot the heave is the stride: `dy = rideOf(stance)·cos((u−stance/2)·4π)·move·0.20 + sin(t·2.2)·0.05` — twice a stride, because the body stands now on one leg and now on the other. **Which way round it goes is decided by whether the stride has a flight phase in it.** While some foot is always down, the legs are **struts**: the body is highest over the middle of a stance, where the strut is straight and under it. Once a stance is under half a stride there is a moment in every stride with no foot down at all, and the leg is a **spring** instead — it comes down onto a foot, bends it, and is thrown back off it — so the body is at its **lowest** over the middle of a stance and highest in the middle of the flight. `rideOf(stance)` is that turnover: 1 at a stance of half a stride or more, −1 at `STANCE_RUN` (0.30), eased in between. A person at 4.7 m/s has a stance of 0.319, so the old heave — always highest at mid-stance — put the body at its top precisely when its leg was taking its weight. Measured off the angles the draw uploads, over one whole stride: a person at 4.7 m/s rises and falls **0.055 m** across a stride, lowest at **0.158** of it (mid-stance is 0.16) and highest at **0.408** (mid-flight is 0.41); at a walk, where the stance is 0.556 and there is no flight phase, the other way round — highest at **0.279** (mid-stance 0.278), lowest at **0.029** (the two feet change over at 0.028). A bear **0.073 m**, an owl **0.046 m**, each at its own speed, and the heave is worth more ground the faster the body goes: 0.025 m at 0.35 of its top speed against 0.055 at 0.94. On the clock it was a sine of its own: a bear at its own speed took 2.8 steps a second while its body came up and down 0.35 times a second. The idle part stays on the clock, and a body with no leg that walks has no stride to ride either. A person's own body had no heave at all and now carries one, on its hips; the limbs it stands on cancel it, which is why the feet stay planted while the body above them rides |
| `pulse` | the frog's throat, the jellyfish bell | a scale, not a rotation |

**A foot that has reached the ground stays there.** The walk cycle used to be one clock for every legged body -- the hip at `sin(t·7)`, the rate a constant -- while the body was driven along by its own speed, and the two had never been spoken to. Read off the matrices the draw uploads, a bear's foot was travelling along the ground at **2.3 m/s** while its body went past at **2.1**, and a person's at **3.0** against **4.1**: a foot was on the ground for four frames in five and held still in none of them, and the whole animal was skating on grass. It is not a tuning problem. A foot sits at the bottom of a sine's arc exactly where that sine is turning over fastest, so the fastest moment of a swing is the moment the foot has to be at its slowest, and no rate fixes that. So the cycle is written as the two acts it has, and the ground keeps the time. `footAt(u)` says where the foot is along its own stride: a straight line from +1 to −1 through the **stance**, because a foot on the ground is being carried along a flat surface by the body and a straight line is the only thing that keeps it there, and an eased curve back from −1 to +1 through the **swing**, which is the only part of a stride a knee may fold in. The share of a cycle that a stance takes is not a constant either -- **62%** at a walk, **30%** at the fastest a body goes -- and the rest of a fast stride is travelled through the air along with the body, which is where a long stride comes from without any leg being swung any wider. The hip angle is then whatever puts the foot at that place, and it is negative because a positive turn about x carries a foot hanging under it toward −z while every model here faces +z. How wide the swing is (`amp = move·stance/0.62`) is the knob that decides how a speed turns into steps, and the two obvious ways to turn it are both wrong: swing wider and a fast body takes long slow strides, keep the swing and shorten the stance and it takes long fast ones, and either way the rate of the steps **falls** as the body goes faster, which is the opposite of what a body does. What a body does is hold its step rate nearly the same and change the length of a step -- a person walking at 1 m/s takes steps of 0.4 m at about 2.5 a second, and running at 4.7 m/s takes steps of 1.9 m at about the same 2.5 -- and making the swing proportional to speed *times the stance* is what makes the two cancel in the one place they have to: a stride comes out proportional to the speed, and the rate of the steps comes out a property of the leg alone. What one cycle is worth along the ground is not a constant either: it is measured, once per model, as how far that model's own sole reaches fore and aft under the body, through the same matrices the draw uploads -- the hinge that sits lowest is not the one the foot hangs from, and the lift that keeps a foot out of the ground moves the whole limb -- and the caller advances the phase by the ground the body covered, so one cycle carries the body exactly that far. Measured over a flat stretch walked at the speed each body walks at: **1.85 m** of stride for a person at 4.7 m/s against the model's own **1.85**, **1.95 m** for a bear at 5.2 against **1.96**, **0.41 m** for an owl against **0.40**; **2.33** steps a second for the person and **2.29** for the bear, and the same rate at 2 m/s with steps under a metre long. And the foot holds: over the frames in which the model says a foot is on the ground, that foot travels **0.006** of the ground the body covers in those same frames for a person and **0.014** for a bear -- the remainder is a foot with no ankle, rocking from toe to heel as the leg sweeps under the body -- where before it was over **1**. A body standing still has no stride at all: its legs stop, its feet move **0 m** in two seconds, and a body walked into a wall stops its legs with its body instead of running on the spot. One number is not right yet and it is on the record rather than hidden: an owl wears **0.4 m** of stride at **6 m/s**, which is **14.9** steps a second and four frames a cycle at this frame rate. The stride is exactly what that body's legs are worth; it is the speed that does not fit the legs, and that is a question about the body's record (04 §4) and not about the cycle.
A part may also declare `air:[rx,ry,rz]` and `crouch:[rx,ry,rz]`: the pose it takes when the body is off the ground, and when its legs are loaded. The weights come from the physics, not from a guess about which animal is being drawn -- `air` is how far off the ground the body actually is, `crouch` how long its feet have been down since the last launch, and `squash` the speed it arrived at the ground with, applied to the whole body as a scale. A body that does not hop is never animated as if it did. A limb the body stands on says so with `stands: 1`, and its off-ground pose then comes only as far as the body is actually off the ground -- `off`, the same 0..1 clearance the caller measures in body heights. Not for tidiness: a hind leg built longer than the body is tall sweeps its foot clean through the plane it stands on on the way into the pose and on the way out of it, because the pose is eased and the body's own origin does not move with it. Read off the draw: a frog landing from a hop drove its shin **0.154 m** into the ground it had just touched, in **169** frames of **360**, with the pose nowhere near the ground at the time.

A part may also declare `swimKick: 1` (this part drives the kick when the body is swimming), `heldPose: [rx,ry,rz]` (the pose it is held in while the water is holding the body up) and `swimPose: [rx,ry,rz]` (the pose it is held in while swimming). `swim` and `held` are the fourth and fifth signals the caller passes. `swim` is a **ratio** rather than a flag -- how near the speed its own record swims at the body is actually moving, and 0 for a body the data gives no swim speed to: **a human in the lake wades**, because the human model has no swim parts and the game has no human stroke. It used to be floored at 0.18, so that a body coasting to a stop kept paddling instead of winding down, and that floor is where the complaint about a hovering frog came from: a body the water held up with nowhere to go walked its feet **1.55 m** through four seconds of standing still. The ratio is handed in by the caller now (`strokeOf`, in the game loop) and it is sized by the water the body is actually pushing, so a body that is going nowhere has no stroke at all and its legs stay in the pose the water holds them in. `held` is the water holding the body up -- in water, breathing it, and not standing on the bottom -- the same reading the physics uses to decide whether a body floats (03 §4), and it is what folds the knee and puts the feet **behind** the body instead of under it, and rolls the whole leg out **away from the centre line** with it. The fold alone leaves a leg bolted to the flank: read off those same matrices, a shin stood **0.063 m** out past the flank of a body **0.169 m** half-wide, with the centre of its foot **0.152 m** off the centre line -- a frog seen from behind was a pair of trousers dropped over the water. Rolled in, the leg went the other way instead: a foot reached **0.115 m** past the centre line of its own body floating and **0.119 m** swimming, both feet pointed in, and the leg came only **0.024 m** and **0.052 m** past the flank -- a frog seen from behind was a pair of legs crossed under itself. Rolled out by 0.1 rad at the hip and 0.1 at the knee, the centre of a foot sits **0.234 m** out on its own side floating and **0.272 m** swimming, no foot ever reaches the centre line (the worst the whole stroke manages is 0.001 m), and the leg stands **0.147 m** and **0.176 m** past the flank; that turn is split over both joints rather than hung on the knee, because the **0.5 rad** the fold used to be given at the knee alone throws the whole leg **0.218 m** past the flank, and a frog floating against the wall of the lake has its feet in the rock. On land the legs stay out at the sides, as the haunch of a frog does: **0.078 m** and **0.181 m**, and that is the pose the water pose is measured against -- the water legs reach more than twice as far out as the land haunch does, which is what a webbed foot is for; the widest they are ever carried is at a slow stroke, where a wild frog crossing the lake takes a leg **0.192 m** past the flank. A body standing in water, dug down on the lakebed or wading where the lake is one block deep, has feet to stand on and stops kicking: a stroke there is a body kicking at nothing. A body the water holds up and that is going nowhere holds its legs still: its foot moves **0 m**, and the pose the water holds it in is the whole of it. A frog does not run on the surface, and it does not paddle a sine either. Both hind legs stroke **together**, one stroke every **1.5 s** (the stroke's own clock is `SWIM_RATE` = 4.2 rad/s) rather than the walk cycle's 7 strides a second, and the stroke is a frog's breaststroke: four keys the joints are eased between -- **gliding**, the pair shut behind the body and the legs run out (knee at **140 deg**, the two feet **0.47 m** apart); **gathered**, the knees drawn apart and the shins folded up against the thighs, so each webbed foot is drawn up beside its own hip, under the rear of the body and never under its middle (knee down to **52 deg**, the pair **0.54 m** across); **driven**, the knees extending and the feet shooting back and in together (**0.17 m** apart, knee run out to **175 deg**, short of straight); and back to the glide, which is what the stroke is for. The angles are offsets from the pose the water already holds the leg in, so a stroke starts and ends where a floating leg already sits. The old cycle was one sine per joint: it ran a foot **0.43 m** fore and aft while the pair stayed **0.43 m** apart through the whole of it -- two legs cycling up and down under a body, shutting no water anywhere -- and seen from behind the animal never changed shape. The front legs fold along the body. The `sign` mirror is dropped for the stroke's timing on purpose -- a synchronous pair is not mirrored in time, and the model data already mirrors the geometry -- but the stroke's sideways turn keeps it, taken from each part's own side, so the whole stroke is one table read twice and the two sides cannot drift out of step with each other the way hand-mirrored data does. Measured on the angles the renderer uploads: on land the two hind thighs sit 1.24 / 1.50 / 1.31 rad apart from each other (alternating), in the water 0.000 (together), and the front legs go from alternating to held at 0.55 rad. Measured in the frame: the silhouette seen from directly behind agrees with its own mirror at **0.965** and **0.957** at the two extremes of one stroke, while those two extremes agree with each other at only **0.559** -- the legs really do move between them. The frame bound is the loose half of the pair -- from directly behind, a leg forward and a leg back move 6% of the outline -- so the claim is also read off the angles themselves, which is where it is actually made. And the foot is followed through the two joint matrices the draw uploads, on the frame a swimming body travels fastest on: its centre runs from **0.26 m behind** the body's rear edge to **0.07 m in front of** it -- still **0.10 m** short of the middle of the body, and never under it -- **0.33 m** of sweep, and it rises **0.19 m**: the gathered foot is the high one, come up to **0.22 m** above the model's own origin, which is the height its own hip hangs it at (**0.23 m**) and well below the back of the body (**0.33 m**), and the driven foot is the low one at **0.04 m**. The knee folds to **52 deg** and runs back out to **175 deg**, never past straight. A wild frog crossing the lake at its own cruising speed sweeps **0.18 m** and keeps its foot **0.17 m** behind that edge at the far end; a body floating with nowhere to go sweeps **0 m** and leaves its feet at the edge; and a body standing in the water keeps the pose it has on dry ground, its foot **0.18 m** in front of the edge either way, the two poses agreeing to within 0.002 m. A turn is kicked with as well as leaned into: the caller hands in `turn` as a **signed** rad/s of heading, and the stroke's sideways turn is scaled by it, so the leg on the outside of the turn takes the longer bite (`TURN_KICK` = 0.7, full at `TURN_FULL` = 1.9 rad/s of heading) and the leg on the inside the shorter one -- a body turning right drives with the leg on its own left, a body turning left with the one on its right. Only the stroke's outward half is scaled (`TURN_FOLD` = 0.3 on the hip's pitch column); the shut is left to the pose, because both feet finish together on the centre line behind the body, and scaling the shut as well drags the outside foot **0.068 m** across it. Read off the matrices the draw uploads, at the frog's own full turn rate: the outside leg sweeps **0.37 m** and carries its foot **1.02 m** through the water, the inside only **0.29 m** and **0.72 m** -- the outside leg sweeps 1.41 times what the inside one does -- and it stands **0.04 m** further out than the inside leg does; its knee still folds to **35 deg**, and its foot still stays behind the body and never under its middle (**0.25 m** of rise against **0.37 m** of sweep). A body steered straight has `turn` exactly 0 and strokes alike on both sides; a body turning left and a body turning right give the same numbers with the two legs exchanged.

**The body itself has two pose signals: `bank` (lean into the turn) and `tilt` (nose along the path).** A body carried by air or by water leans into its turn and points along the course it is actually taking; a body on foot does neither, because feet grip and the ground answers. Neither is a part animation — the renderer rotates the whole body, first about its own facing axis, then about its own lateral axis — and both are one rule and one set of numbers for the player's body and for every animal's. The lean is the sideways push the arc needs over the pull a wing works against: `atan(turn rate × speed / 9.8)`, clamped to 0.45 rad, eased at 4.5. The game's own gravity is 23 m/s² because a jump has to read, and it is deliberately *not* the number here, because a lean is not a fall. The pitch is the same idea turned sideways: `atan(−climb rate / speed)`, clamped to 0.6, eased at 4.5, nose-down positive, the climb rate being the height the body just gained or lost. Measured: a body swimming a fixed course peaks at **0.427 rad** (24°) and a flying one at **0.445** (25°); the same two bodies steered straight peak at **0**; a bear driven through the same quarter turn stays at **0**; a body letting go of its height settles at **0.542** nose-down, a body climbing at **−0.31**, a body on foot at **0**; and with the pitch taken out of the draw, **0** of the **517** pixels those frames change are left. Two exemptions, and both are bodies: a bird asleep on a branch turns itself in place, and that is not a lean; a bell that pulses has no nose to point, so the lamp jellyfish leans and never pitches. The turn rate handed to the lean is **signed**, and it used not to be: the lean was built from the absolute value of the heading change, so every body banked the same way whichever way it turned -- a body turning left leaned into the turn and a body turning right leaned out of it, and the two legs' kicks had nothing honest to be read against. That rate carries a dead band of **0.12 rad/s** as well: a heading read twice a frame off a body crawling through water and brushing its banks jitters by a degree or two, and a lean built on that jitter weaves the shoulders of a body swimming dead straight. Measured: a body steered straight now holds `turn` at exactly 0 across all **94** frames of its run, and a body driven through a quarter turn peaks at **1.46 rad/s** with its two legs' kicks mirrored to within **0.02**.

**Nothing with a body turns on the spot.** A body the air or the water is holding bends the course it is already on, at `turn` radians a second — a field on the animal record and the same number on the form record of the same species, because the animal and the body worn from it are one body: owl 1.4, frog 1.9, brown bear 1.6, lamp jellyfish 0.8, human 1.6. Above its own cruising speed the arc gets wider rather than the turn getting faster, because the same push has more body to swing about; and the rate is capped as well at 2.2 × how far off course the body is, which is what stops a body weaving when the thing it is aimed at and the way it is going disagree. Measured before: a frogpear in the water opened a turn at **94 rad/s** and crossed the lake at **9.9 m/s**, against the 4.6 m/s its own record swims at; an owlberry flew at **4.2 m/s** against the 7.0 it flies at, and came about 90° in **1.03 s**. After: peak **1.90** and **1.40 rad/s**, and **4.63** and **6.97 m/s** held to within 0.04 of the record the whole way round a circle; the quarter turn in **1.77 s**. The course really is an arc: the frog's path measures **3.16 m** along and **2.98 m** straight across, the owl's **13.43** along and **12.17** across, while a body steered straight measures 3.16 along and 3.16 across. A body on foot is deliberately *not* arc-limited: feet grip, and a body on foot turning is the answer to the mouse, not to a rudder.

**An animal that cannot make the turn slows for it.** The arc of a turn is speed over rate, so a place nearer than the arc, or off to one side, is a place the animal circles and never arrives at. The speed that puts the circle through the place comes from the geometry: the way to the place is a chord of the circle, and a chord that meets the heading at the course error belongs to a circle whose radius is half the distance over the sine of that error. The naive bound — the distance over the sine, without the half — is exactly the speed at which the animal circles for ever at its own arc width: an owl sent to a place 1.8 m to one side walked **73 m** round it in 25 seconds and never came inside **1.79 m** of it. Measured on the same three errands after: the owl stands **0.8 m** from that place having walked **1.9 m**, a frog **0.79 m** from a place 1.2 m off having walked **1 m** (before: 48 m round it), a lamp jellyfish **0.8 m** from one 0.9 m off having walked **0.4 m** (before: 23 m round it). Out in the open, where the place is far and the sine is small, the rule costs nothing; near it, it reads as an animal easing as it comes about, which is what it is.

**An animal that meets a wall goes round it.** Of the four steps it can take it takes the one that stands and points most the way it wants to go, and the heading follows the step at the animal's own turn rate — the contour of the obstacle rather than a fresh errand. Giving up was the whole of the old behaviour: the two bears of the northern ridge walked in **20%** of frames and covered **24 m** between them in ninety seconds, because in front of a cliff the step was rejected, the errand dropped, and the same rock walked into again (in a closer look at the same two bears the step in front of them was rejected **2024** times in **2400** frames). After: **94%** of frames and **51 m**; and the frogs, which meet the same walls all along the lakeshore, went from **76%** to **93%**. One turn budget a frame, whoever spent it: steering at the errand and stepping round the obstacle each bent the heading by the full allowance, and a bear at a cliff came about at **3.2 rad/s** against the **1.6** its body allows.

**The wing beat belongs to the climb.** `flap` is the fifth signal the caller passes, 0 to 1: a flying body that is not asking for height eases its stroke out to **0.15** and rides its wings, and the body comes down while the wings stop pushing (**0.163** measured descending, **0.999** the moment the climb is asked for). The stroke and the heave the renderer uploads go with that signal rather than with the clock: a full beat takes the wing through **1.739 rad** and lifts the body **0.16 m**, the riding beat takes the wing through **1.383 rad** and the body **0.024 m**. The animals carry the same signal — an owl coming down eases out, an owl climbing beats full, a bird asleep on a branch holds its wings folded at **0**.

**Feet that are on a surface are standing on it.** The contact flag used to be lit only by a move the collision had to stop, and a body is parked 0.0005 m off the face it last hit, so a frame has to sink that far before the cell below is sampled again. Gravity covers it on land -- 0.006 m in a frame -- so the flag only flickers out for a frame at a time and the eased pose hides it. In water it cannot: buoyancy cancels most of the weight and the drag leaves a terminal sink of about 0.05 m/s, 0.0008 m a frame, so the bottom is sampled once every three frames. A body wading where the lake is one block deep -- and the shore of Mirrorlake is 183 such columns -- was then reported airborne the whole time it stood on the bottom: the air pose eased up to 0.42 and stayed there, and the steering kept a third of its push (accel 12 against 34). So after the displacement, feet within 2 cm of a solid and the body not rising means it is standing; 2 cm is the distance the step-up already uses to settle a body back onto the ledge it climbed, and far less than the fall a body picks up once it has left a surface (0.006 m in the first frame, 0.10 m by the fifth). Measured: standing in such a column the body is on the bottom **100%** of frames with the pose at **0** -- before, 50% and 0.42 -- and wading along the shelf it covers **5.19 m** in two seconds with its feet down; and the flag is not glue, a body that walks off a single step is off the ground for **18** of the next 40 frames, pose eased to **0.88**, having dropped **1.0 m**.

**A limb with no ankle lifts, and a limb that stands refuses to be carried down.** A thigh that swings, or a knee that folds, carries the corner of the foot that points the way of the swing down through the line the foot rests on. Nothing about the gait is wrong when that happens -- the model is simply missing a joint, and the limb has to make up for it by lifting. So every part that turns in the land gait, and whose limb reaches the ground line, carries the corners of the boxes it turns, its own and every part hanging under it (a thigh that swings carries the foot at the end of it), measured from its hinge; and it is lifted by as much as its own turn drives those corners below where they rest. The lift goes to the **top** of the limb, so the whole limb moves together and the joint between thigh and shin does not open. The other half is the heave: the body rides its own stride as it walks (`bob`, ±0.25 of the bear's own units at its fastest -- 0.20 of stride and 0.05 of idle breath) and the limbs hang off it, so a body that dips takes its feet with it -- 6 cm into the floor, twice a stride, on every step. A limb standing on the ground does not go down when the body above it comes up, so it cancels that heave, and only the top of the limb cancels it, or the answer is counted twice. Only limbs that reach the ground line are given the table: a forearm swings, and drops, and nothing about it should move. The lift is one-way for a turn -- a foot may rise in mid-swing, it may not go under -- and two-way for the heave. Read off the draw over 180 frames of walking and 120 of standing on one flat clearing: a bear's hind foot reached **0.091 m** under the floor in **32** frames of its walk, an owl's toe **0.063 m** in **106** of 180 and **0.055 m** in **17** of 120, a person's boot **0.032 m** in **32**, a frog's shin **0.164 m** in **169** of 360. After: **0.000 m** for the bear and the person, **0.007 m** for the frog, **0.025 m** in one frame for the owl. Which half closes which: the knee's quarter-cycle lag closes the bear and the frog, the lift closes the person's boot (**0.033 m** with it switched off) and the owl's walk (**0.064 m**), and the pitch rule below closes the owl's standing (**0.071 m**). And a lift must not carve a limb off the body it hangs from, so the same step measures the gap between a part and the part above it: every body sits at **0** against a 5 mm bound, and carving the owl's leg short to prove the check works left a **1.2 cm** slit of sky where the hip should be. A heave is bounded the same way, by how deep a leg is carved into the part it hangs from: a person's thigh boxes reached only 0.1 of their own units -- 1.8 cm -- into the hips, and a body that rides its stride by 0.25 units at that scale needs 4.5 cm of overlap, so they were carved to 0.3 units. The extra 2 cm sits inside the hips box, in the same colour, and is never seen.

**A body built for the air is on foot the moment its feet are on the ground.** The pitch a body wants is the angle of the path it is taking, `atan(−climb rate / max(speed, 0.5))`, and the slower the body goes the worse that question reads: the wing branch answers "in the air" for the whole frame whatever the collision said a moment ago, so a bird standing on the ground is handed one frame of gravity (−0.38 m/s) against a speed of 0 and reads it as a dive -- `atan(0.38 / 0.5)` is **0.65 rad**. So the body asks the ground instead of the flag: how far above the level it would be standing at in this column it is, `y − standY(...) < 0.02` m, the same 2 cm the step-up and the water's hold use -- and it asks *before* the vertical branch runs, not after. And the frame the ground arrests the body's fall it arrests the body's turn as well: the landing sets the pitch to **0** in the same frame it sets the squash. Measured off the draw, an owl standing still held **0.60 rad** of nose-down for as long as it stood and its toes reached **0.071 m** under the floor it stood on; walking, **0.055 m**. After: pitch **0** standing, **0** walking, toes **0.030 m** standing and **0.023 m** walking, and the frames that remain are the last moment of a fall -- the body a couple of centimetres above the surface, coming in at 0.42 rad, its toes through the surface for one frame before the body lands on it. A body still in the air keeps its nose along the path it is taking: **−0.55** climbing, **0.44** falling.

**A body built for the air lies along the way it is going.** The pitch above is the angle of the path, and it is not the posture: a body can be pointed along a level course and still be standing up while it crosses it. The owl was. It is carved standing, because standing wedged in a hollow is what it does by day, and it kept that posture in the air: read off the matrices the draw uploads, an owl crossing the valley at 7 m/s held its spine **72.6°** up from the horizontal climbing, **72.8°** gliding and **76.7°** diving, its head **87°** above its own body and its feet **0.24 m** under its centre — the same body that stands on a branch, with wings bolted on. The posture cannot be asked of the caller's one number, because that number is already the path: if it carried the posture as well then a level flight would have no posture at all, which is exactly what it had. So a part may declare `fly:[rx,ry,rz]` — the pose of a body the air is holding up — asked for by the caller's `fly` flag and carried by the same eased `air` the wings and the tucked legs ride, so a bird coming off a branch lies down over a few frames instead of snapping flat in one. The hinge is the hip the legs hang from, so the legs and the tail go where a body turning nose-over-heels takes them instead of being left behind. Measured on the same matrices, at the three moments of a flight: spine **36.0°** climbing, **13.2°** gliding, **5.3°** diving, and **90.0°** for the same bird standing on the ground — the pose it was carved in, which is why the blend is asked of the air under the body and not of the shape of it. The head answers back: a body laid 72° over would otherwise spend the whole of its flight looking at the ground it is hunting over, so the face comes back to **5°** under the horizon. The tail is carved at the rear of the trunk, which is where a standing bird carries it and the wrong place for a flying one: laid over, that rear corner is on top and the tail flies like a flag; with nothing said the fan lies **27°** above the horizon while the body it hangs from lies **18°** above it, at **−0.95** it droops **28°** below the horizon and **46°** under the line of the body, and **−0.45** leaves it **1°** above the horizon and **18°** under that line — a folded tail streaming behind. The legs need no pose of their own: the `air` tuck they already carry, turned by the body lying down, becomes a fold under the breast — **0.21 m** behind the body's centre and **0.11 m** under it — and giving them a pose of their own turned them into two sticks. The body's own box turns with it: in the air **0.58 m** tall against **0.64 m** long where it had been **0.73** tall against **0.50**, and standing stays **0.71** by **0.46**. And the wild bird takes the pose over the frames it is leaving its branch: the flag that decides it used to flip in a single frame, which flipped the wings, the tucked legs and the whole length of the body with it — measured, **1.0** of the posture in one frame, against **0.183** now.

A model carries **no scale of its own**. One number sizes a body: the collision box in `data/creatures.js` (`size`, in metres). The model is authored in unit boxes, and the creature's `scale` turns them into metres — so the model and the box are the same body twice, and if they disagree the player is clipped by geometry they can see themselves not touching. `game/test/node-smoke.js` measures every model against the box its creature declares and fails the build when it overhangs: the model's horizontal radius may exceed the box's half-width by at most 0.1 m, its top the box's height by at most 0.06 m. A **radius**, not a width, because the box is a square footprint that does not turn with the body — a model that fits face-on still sticks through a wall sideways. Before the gate existed the bear reached 1.26 m from its centre while its box was 0.70 m across: 0.56 m of bear inside the wall it was leaning against, and the frog's snout 0.13 m past its own box.

The lamp jellyfish is the one model with **no box to be measured against**: nothing in the game collides with it (you swim through the lamps), and it bears no fruit, so no player ever wears it. It is checked against the size the codex gives it instead — **0.61 m across, 0.74 m from the top of the dome to the tip of the longest arm**, measured at its `scale` of 0.16.

5 models: human, owl, frog, bear, jelly.

## 2. Audio direction (not implemented, full design)

**No soundtrack, only "ambience + material".**

| Layer | Content |
|---|---|

| Creatures | the chorus after rain (a 12-part frog loop; when the player is a frog it joins as the 13th part), single owl notes, low harmonies under water (only audible when submerged) |

| UI | codex page turn = paper; cellar door = wood |
| Danger | inside the Grey Veil every sound is "sucked away" (the low-pass cutoff falls to 400 Hz with depth) |

**The audio rule during transformation**: at the instant of transformation, the call of the creature matching the current form plays centred on the player and continues for the whole form duration (volume varies with movement). This makes the player *hear what they are*.

### 3.2 Rendering

- **Chunks**: 16×48×16, 10×10 = 100 chunks. Each chunk has 6 meshes: `[opaque, emissive, water, props, prop-emissive, glass]`.
- **Face culling**: faces adjacent to a solid opaque neighbour are not generated.
- **Frustum culling**: 6 planes extracted from the MVP (row-major rows: `row3 ± row0/1/2`), tested against a **box** per chunk -- the chunk's own 16 x 48 x 16, and against each plane only the one corner of it that points furthest along that plane's normal, which is all a box ever has to answer. It used to be a sphere of radius **15** centred at half the map's height, and a chunk's own corners are **26.5** out from that centre, so a chunk whose only piece on screen was one of them was thrown away whole and the sky showed through the hole. A lens just above Mirrorlake, looking down at 69 degrees, asked the world what stood in front of it and found nothing drawn on **56,633** of the pixels that had an answer: a grey wedge of dry sky at the far corner of a chunk of lakebed, exactly where the lakebed is deepest. Two other lenses over the same water put **75,369** and **42,186** pixels of the same hole in the same frame. The test is a frame against itself -- the same lens drawn with the culling the game runs, and drawn with none at all -- and the two now agree byte for byte at eight lenses, which is worth saying because those eight do throw whole chunks away: between **40** and **88** of the valley's 100 a frame.
- **Emissive layer**: a separate mesh, raised by `emisBoost`, unaffected by day/night lighting.
- **Water**: a separate mesh, and it carries no alpha at all. The surface used to be a sheet of coloured glass laid over the frame: `BLOCKS[WATER].alpha` said **0.72**, folded once into the fourth byte of the vertex colour by `def()` and never written a second time at the draw call, and at 0.62 the lakebed kept 38% of its own contrast through it and the lake read as a pit in the ground rather than as water. It is gone. What water does to light is one law, per colour: `fogK: [1.40, 0.40, 0.15]` on the block row says how fast this stuff eats daylight per metre, colour by colour, and the same law answers every journey light makes through a lake. That row used to say `[0.55, 0.16, 0.06]` -- the absorption of a mountain tarn -- and from its bank this lake was an aquarium: a lens 40 cm over the surface picked the blocks of a bed 6 m down out of the water, the bed keeping **0.29** of the spread it has when it is dry, and the whole floor of Mirrorlake was legible from the shore. The green is the channel the eye reads brightest and the one that decides how far down one can see, and it now eats **0.40** per metre, which puts this lake's own visibility near **2.5 m**: from the bank you see water, with a suggestion of a floor under it. The ratio between the three colours is the one the row has always had -- that ratio is why the floor is teal and not grey -- only how fast the whole thing happens was wrong. (The numbers quoted further down this file were measured through this lake: the ones the current test log still produces are re-pinned where they stand, and the rest are the record of the day each of them was caught.) The sun's way down, baked into the bed's vertices as `aUnder`. The way back up, asked of whatever surface the lens is looking at, and measured along the ray the light travels rather than straight up. The water the lens itself sits in, which is the haze. And the water between the lens and the surface it is looking through, asked of that face. The lake used to answer that one question three times over -- the alpha, a haze copied from the air's squared law at 0.14 per metre, and the baked absorption -- and the three disagreed by **48** luminance at two metres of water. The alpha was also the only thing the lake had between a lens and the sky, so it was drawn only when the lens was out of the water and cost exactly nothing from under it: it hid a bright shallow bed while brightening a dark deep one. Vertex waves: `sin(t*1.7 + x*0.9 + z*0.7)*0.055 + sin(t*0.6 + z*0.25)*0.03`. The surface is drawn **un-culled** and **twice**. Un-culled because it is one quad per block face, so seen from underneath it is a back face and the culler -- on for the whole frame -- threw it away: standing on the bed of a three-block-deep column, the brightest pixel in the frame was **255** and **2.73%** of it was over 200/255, which was the sun seen straight through the lake; now **128** and **0%**. Twice because how much of the light survives is per colour while a blend can only scale the whole frame by one number: the first pass multiplies the frame by `survival x (1 - F)`, the second adds what the face throws back and what the water scatters into the ray on its way out. A lake is a closed volume and the mesher only emits a face where water meets air, so no pixel can ever see both sides of the same quad: no double blending, no extra geometry.
- **A lake is a mirror from the bank and a window from a boat**: the surface's reflectance is Fresnel, `0.02 + 0.98 * pow(1 - cn, 5)`, asked of the face the lens is looking at rather than of the lens, so the same water throws most of its view back when it is looked at along and lets nearly all of it through when it is looked at down. Two lenses 30 cm above one three-metre shelf, at noon, over the pixels the water covers: the grazing one mean luminance **94** against the steep one's **59.8**, and the grazing one is also the bluer (**125.7** against **93**) because what comes back off it is the sky. The dry bed under the same two lenses is the other way round -- **171.6** and **178.7**, blue **126.3** and **129.8** -- which is the whole of the difference between a surface and a floor.
- **Glass**: `BLOCKS[GLASS].alpha` has said 0.34 since the first commit and nothing ever read it. Glass rode in the opaque mesh, so the castle greenhouse, the windows of the sunken tower and every window in Lanternrow were painted blue-grey slabs you could not see through and could not walk into. A block that is neither opaque nor liquid now rides a sixth mesh of its own, drawn after the bodies so the depth they wrote decides whether a body shows through a window, and its alpha arrives as the fourth byte of the vertex colour. Faces between two neighbouring blocks of the same transparent kind are still culled, so a wall of glass is one pane thick rather than one pane per brick.
- **The lake has a colour from the shore**: the water surface used to be the only blended draw in the game, and the world shader divided the alpha byte by 255 *after* the attribute pointer had already normalized it, which left the surface 0.4% opaque. Standing outside Mirrorlake you saw no lake at all, and the only water with a colour was the fog you breathe with your head under. Every other test reads the world; this one has to read the frame: draw, draw again with nothing the water does left anywhere in the frame -- the law, the haze, the colour the water scatters back into the ray and the surface itself, which leaves the bed as dry sand -- and the two frames must differ, over a tenth of the picture (**49,621** pixels), and differ towards the water's own blue (**47,856** of those pixels bluer than red with the water and not without it). The claim is the colour, not the brightness: over the pixels the water covers, the bed's red falls to **0.17** of what it is dry, its green to **0.45** and its blue keeps **0.711**. A tint painted over the frame takes the same fraction off every channel; the lake does not, and that is the difference between water and shade. The older question -- how much of the bed's own spread survives the surface -- meant nothing when it was asked, because the lake was an aquarium then: the surface put as much variation into the picture as the bed lost, so over a three-metre shelf the wet frame was **2.7** times as varied as the dry bed under it. Asked again with the row eating at the rate it eats now, that question is the one that decides whether a lake hides its floor, and it is asked of the same bed with nothing the water does left anywhere in the frame: a bed 6 m down keeps **0.045** of the spread it has dry, a bed 4 m down keeps **0.297**.
- **Sky**: a full-screen quad, procedural gradient + sun/moon discs + stars. The quad rebuilds a view direction per pixel from the camera basis — `vDir = normalize(uFwd + uRight·aCorner.x·tanX + uUp·aCorner.y·tanY)` — so the basis the renderer hands the shader has to be the same handedness the view matrix was built from. It was not: `begin()` computed `up = fwd × right`, which is `right × fwd` (what `M4.lookYaw` uses) negated. Only the sky reads `up`, so every block, body and particle was where it should be while the sky alone was mirrored about the horizon: the top of the screen sampled *below* the horizon and got the ground colour, and the sun's glow sat under the horizon. At 07:00, lens level, facing the sun's azimuth with the sun 31° up, the brightest pixel in a 300 px frame sat 135 px below the centre. Stars are gated on the sun being **down** (`smoothstep` on `-uSunDir.y`), not on `uNight`: the day/night curve is symmetric about noon, so at 07:00 `uNight` still says 37% night while the sun is 29° up, and 217 star spikes were measured in a daylight sky next to the sun.
- **Fog**: exponential, colour follows day/night; underwater changes colour and density — and "underwater" is read off the **lens's** own cell, not the body's head; inside the Grey Veil it becomes dense grey fog.
- **The dark of a night is the sky's light**: at midnight every surface was lit by `uDay` alone — 0.16 of its own colour — and by nothing else, so a dark material (conifer `[0.14, 0.30, 0.20]`, dark leaf, roof tile) came out at luminance 7–10 while the night sky behind it was 17–21: a tree or a roof read as a hole cut in the sky. Measured on four night lenses: **4–42%** of the frame below luminance 12, darkest surface **6.8**, sky **17.3–21.3**; the same four lenses at 13:00 have **0%** below 12. The sky's own colour is now **added** to every surface (`uAmb`, from `env.ambient`), gated on the sun being properly down — the gate the stars already use, which is also where the sun's own light ends (`day` reaches 0 at an elevation of −0.1375), so dusk is left alone. Added rather than multiplied, because that is the shape the problem has: it lifts a dark surface by most of its own value and a bright one by hardly anything. After: **0%** below 12 on all four lenses, darkest surface **15.3–17.5** against a sky of **18–21.3**; and a daytime frame is bit for bit what it was, because `ambient` is `[0,0,0]` from 13:00 through 18:00.
- **The bottom of a lake is under water**: the water put a haze between the lens and a surface, and that was all it did -- and a haze works over distance, so at the lake's density a fragment 3 m away took 18% of it and one 10 m away took 86%. The lakebed a body was swimming over therefore kept the dry sand colour it has on the shore. Measured looking straight down from just under the surface, the frame was **warmer than red** (blue-minus-red **-16.7**) and **brighter** (mean **137**) than the same lens looking *up* at the sunlit surface (mean **90**) -- the bottom of a lake brighter than its own sunlit skin. What was missing is that the light reaching a surface under water has itself come down through the water above it: the red goes first. `BLOCKS[WATER]` now carries `fogK: [1.40, 0.40, 0.15]` -- how fast this stuff eats daylight, per metre, colour by colour -- and the shader multiplies a surface's daylight by `exp(-fogK x depth under the surface)`. **The depth is the surface's own, not the lens's.** For the static world it is measured when the mesh is built: `buildChunk` walks every column of the chunk **plus a ring of one**, up front, and keeps each column's surface height and its lowest wet cell in two small typed arrays -- the same columns the lazy walk ended up visiting anyway, since every face vertex asks for its own column and the four it shares a face with, and a horizontal face asks for the column under the block it belongs to, which stands on that corner's diagonal -- and every vertex inside that span is uploaded with how far under it sits, in 1/8 m steps, as a third byte beside the shade and the emissive flag (`aUnder`). **Only 18 of the valley's 100 chunks hold water, and the 82 that do not are now handed no water question at all**: they were paying five map lookups for every one of their vertices to be told nothing. A chunk rebuild cost **4.15 ms** on average and **15.4 ms** in the worst chunk, and one fruit picked in that chunk cost **22.5 ms** of frame; after, **0.97 ms** and **1.7 ms**, and **1.7 ms** for that harvest. `game/test/play.js` reads the depth byte back off the meshes the mesher uploads and checks every one of them against the lake, column by column (**0** wrong of **31,589** vertices that carry water), shows that **398** of them could only have come from a column in the next chunk over, and counts the map lookups that sweep pays over the whole valley: **0** for the **1,096,650** vertices it uploads, where the same sweep used to pay **15,982,908** of them. The valley rebuilds in **102 ms** -- **0.09 ms** per thousand vertices, against **508 ms** and **0.46 ms** -- and no chunk in it takes more than the **2.2 ms** its worst one does, against the 6 ms a frame gives meshing. **That column question is the wrong question for one kind of face, and a lakebed is made of it.** A face the mesher can see standing against water hands its surface height in instead, because a corner of a submerged face can sit inside the solid next door, where no column of water reaches down to it: the point question then answers "dry", and the corner is lit the colour of a beach. 644 of the 5,366 faces this mesher puts against water had at least one corner answered that way -- 1,619 corners, the worst at (74, 5, 95) with the lake's surface 6 m above it -- and in the frame they were the strange bright dots on the lakebed: a lens just above the surface, looking down, sees 70,980 pixels of bed through at least three metres of water, and **137** of them came back warmer than blue, **35** brighter than 140, the brightest **176** against a bed whose own average is 60. Asked of the face instead, every corner is answered and every one of those 137 is gone: **0** warmer than blue, **0** over 140, brightest **64**. A vertex still takes the deepest of its own column and its four neighbours, which is what makes the face of a rock the lake covers wet while the roof of a cave under the lakebed is not. The coefficient itself reaches the frame once, as `env.liqK` off `liquidAbsorb()`. For the things that are not baked -- a body, whose vertices are rebuilt every frame, and a fruit dropped on the lakebed -- the draw is handed the surface of the column *that thing* stands in (`liquidSurfaceAt()`, and for a body its own `wSurf`, which it collects in its own update); the outline box round the block you are looking at is handed none, because it lights itself, and a thing that lights itself is not lit by the sun coming down through the lake. The world's own meshes are handed none of it either: their depth came in with them. **The world owns the reading of a column**: `liquidSurfaceAt()` is its surface as a height and `waterTop()` in the physics file is the same column as a *cell*, and both answer **-1** for a column holding no water, so the two conventions cannot disagree about whether a column is in the lake at all -- swept over the valley, **2,029** wet columns and **2,327** dry ones, and none in disagreement. The exponent is linear in the depth, and deliberately not the haze's squared-exponential law: the haze is scattering over the viewing path (nothing in the first metre, everything at a hundred), while the sunlight coming down is being absorbed, and absorption starts eating the red from the first metre -- which is exactly where a body wading over a four metre shelf is looking. **After**, at noon, a lens 60 cm under the surface of a six-metre shelf: looking down, blue-minus-red **+61.9** at mean **45.9**; looking up at the sunlit skin, **+112.7** at mean **69.9** -- the skin brighter than the floor, which is the order it has to be in; and on a bed 9 m down, **+55.1** at mean **46.2**. **The floor at arm's length is the same question, and the haze cannot answer it**: the lens there is standing on the bank, out of the water, so there is no haze between it and that bed at all -- what reaches the bed is the one law asked of the surface the lens is looking through. Measured on the patch straight in front of the crosshair, not over the whole frame, which averages the far lake in with it: a bed two metres down reads blue over red by **+80** at mean luminance **57**. With the law switched off the same patch is red over blue by **69** at mean **187** -- the colour of a lakebed that has never been under water. The same patch with the bed five metres deeper reads **+58** at mean **46**: it keeps losing red as the floor goes down while its blue holds, which is the difference between a law and a tint. **And what is left down there is the veil, not the floor**: the same column drawn one depth at a time, the lens 40 cm over its surface, and the frame drawn again with nothing the water does left anywhere in it -- which is the only way to ask how much of a floor survives without asking how bumpy that particular floor is. The spread across the patch is what the eye reads as relief, because a floor is blocks and water is not, and it falls to **0.297** of the dry bed's at four metres down and to **0.045** of it at six; and the six-metre patch has come to within a luminance of the colour the water scatters back into the ray (**48.5** against the water's own **49.7**) while the four-metre one still stands **3** above it. **The way back up is measured along the ray, not straight up**: that leg used to be the bed's own depth under the surface -- a vertical -- while the haze a lens sits in has always been measured along the ray, and which of the two a frame gets is decided by `Math.floor(lens.y)`, which flips at the surface line. A lens 5 cm under a four-metre shelf, looking at its bed along 7 degrees of slope, wore the whole veil; 5 cm over the same spot it was handed only the vertical depth, and the murk left the frame as the lens crossed the line. Measured on the bed 2-5 m in front of the crosshair, 3-4 m down, at noon: mean luminance and blue over red **69.8 / 80.6** with the lens 5 cm under the line, **81.3 / 71.3** at the line, **87.4 / 72.6** 5 cm over it and **88.6 / 67.0** 30 cm over -- the bed drier the higher the lens climbed, which is the bug -- and the bed's own red back at **31.3** and **42.4** where under the line it is **19.3**. The vertical is now put along the ray (`vert * d / abs(vEye.y)`, capped at the ray's own length: a four-metre slab looked at along 17 degrees of slope is 13 m of water, and the ray never walks 13 m of it). After, at the coefficient the row carries today: **51.2 / 69.7** under the line, **51.4 / 69.8** at it, **59.3 / 70.6** 5 cm over, **61.5 / 70.1** 30 cm over, with the bed's red at **15 / 23.3 / 25.3** for those same three lenses. Looked at nearly straight down, where the two measures are all but one and the same line, nothing moves across the boundary: **50.2 / 50.4 / 50.4**. And the lamps: the shader discounts the haze for a fragment that lights itself, so that a lantern across the valley is still a lantern -- and that discount was a constant **0.75** written in the shader, in which the lake had no say. The lamp jellyfish sit on the outline of the sunken tower, so a body that swims in among them faces a ring of them: 3.6 m off that ring the frame came back with pixels at luminance **246**, its 99th percentile at **180** and **0.72%** of it over 200/255 -- a sun sitting on the lakebed. The discount is now `fogEmis` on the water row (**0.15**), reaching the shader as `env.emisKeep`, with the air's 0.75 as the default so a frame drawn in air is bit for bit what it was. After, at the coefficient the row carries today: that ring of lamps at mean **49.2**, its 99th percentile **101**, and nothing at all over 200/255.
- **Particles**: a single draw call, capped at 900 (glowing seeds, splashes, transformation smoke). A mote is not a surface: it is *added* to the frame, so the water cannot colour it over -- what the water does to it is eat part of the light the mote sends out on its way to the lens, and until now a mote was asked nothing at all. Sixty bubbles let go 0.4-2.9 m under Mirrorlake, 4-8 m from a lens sitting 60 cm under the surface of the same lake, came back over **429** sampled pixels at mean luminance **140**, **197** of those pixels over 140 and the brightest **222**, while the lakebed behind them, at the same lens, reads **66..70** -- the same strange bright dot the lakebed used to be, still floating there after the lakebed itself had been fixed. After: **303** pixels, mean **101**, their red **69** down to **20**, brightest **150**, and **15** over 140. (Those numbers were taken with the coefficient the water row carried that day. The row eats faster now, and a mote under the lake is dimmer than that record says -- what has not moved is the ratio the law keeps between a mote seen through the lake and the same mote with the law switched off, which is what the step measures today: a mote 5.5 m down keeps **4** of its red where the same mote with the law off keeps **127**, and its brightest contribution falls from **171** to **67**.) How far a mote sits under the surface of its own water arrives as an attribute the way the static world's depth does, measured at the moment of drawing rather than at spawn, because a bubble rises and the lake it has already climbed out of is gone. And the walk is measured **along the ray**: a bubble hung 2.5 m under the surface keeps **32** of its red looked at from straight above, and **2** looked at along 20 degrees of slope from the same distance -- 7 m of lake rather than 2.5. The water the lens itself stands in asks the same question: a mote 8 m off inside the lake keeps **2** of its red where one 2 m off keeps **42**, and a splash a metre over the skin keeps **20** seen from 3 m down against **70** seen from 30 cm down. A mote in the air seen from the air walks no water at all: exp(0) is 1, and the frame it is drawn into is byte for byte the frame that was drawn before this law existed. A bubble also pops where it reaches the surface instead of going on through the open air: it is the one buoyant thing the valley spawns (`grav < 0` -- motes and dust hover at 0, embers fall at 1.2), and it used to rise clean out of the lake and float on for the rest of its life. Ten seconds of a body whose head sits 30 cm under the surface of a four metre shelf: **2,770** of **3,834** bubble-samples were **above** the surface of the lake, the highest **5.84** m over the water; the same ten seconds with the body standing on the bed four metres down, **1,455** of **3,261**, up to **3.68** m over it. After: none, and the cell test that decides it costs one read per mote per tick. The span the spawner scatters bubbles over is the water the body stands in now -- feet first, no higher than 25 cm under the skin -- so a bubble is not born in the open air only to be killed a tick later (**28** of **47** births were in water before, **30** of **43** after). The night's motes are put down by the game rather than found in the world, and the column read that puts them down answers "the highest solid thing in this column" -- over a lake that is the lakebed: ninety seconds standing on the shore of Mirrorlake let go **28** of **76** fireflies under the surface of the lake and **16** inside a block, and **34%** of the motes the night kept alive glowed through up to **8.4 m** of water. The surface of the water is the floor to lift off from where there is one (`liquidSurfaceAt`, and the higher of the two answers wins), and a column with no room left above its highest solid leaves the mote unmade rather than buried. And a mote keeps to the air it was put in. A mote is drawn, not walked: it has no size and no mass, and the frame it is added into has already been depth-tested, so a mote inside a block is not seen *through* the block -- it is simply not there, holding a slot off the 900 and fading where nobody can see it. The valley was spending a great deal of its light that way: standing on a lake shore at night, **703** of **12,917** mote-samples were inside a block; the dust of the highest isle **20,793** of **65,627** (**31.7%**), with **87** of its **371** grains already inside a block at the moment they were born, because the span they are scattered over is a box and the isle is standing in it; a spark burst thrown at the ground it stands on buried **286** of **4,109** samples under that ground, and **426** of **4,080** thrown up under a crown. The world is a wall to a mote the way it is a wall to a body: the move into a solid is refused and the mote spends itself against the face it reached -- one read, the same one the bubble rule above already pays -- while a mote the world has already closed around keeps its move, so that it can still work its way out (**19** frames out of a needle). And the ring the night's motes are scattered over reaches eighteen metres out from the body, which near the edge of the map is off the map, where `get` answers "air" to a question that was never asked: standing eight blocks in from the edge, **85** of **682** fireflies were let go outside the valley and **21,863** of **149,080** samples (**14.7%**) were spent there, glowing at nothing. After: none of these, at any of the four spots, and the dust is let go at the first air above the beams instead of in a box the isle stands in.
- **Camera**: the lens points exactly where the mouse points, in both views. What trails behind is the body (`faceYaw`), which has mass. A lens that eased toward the aim was the bug: W ran off in a direction the screen was not pointing at, and the highlight box sat over a block the crosshair was not on. `cam.yaw` / `cam.pitch` are gone — the only camera state left is the distance and the field of view, both eased.
- **Third-person distance**: `1.5 + 2.25 × height`, eased, plus 8° of field of view — the subject keeps roughly the same share of the frame whether it is a frog or a bear.
- **Occlusion**: a DDA from the subject toward the lens, solid blocks only (fruit and reeds must not blind the camera). The lens is backed off to the hit block's **near face** minus 0.2 m, never closer than 0.55 m.
- **Inside your own body**: pulled in that far, the lens is inside the box it is filming, so the body is not drawn — otherwise you are looking at the inside of your own skull.
- **The view follows the lens, the body keeps breathing**: a third-person lens trails the body, and it trails it *down*. Standing on a shore shelf with the lake surface at y = 15 and a dry head at 16, the lens sat at **14.19** — under the water. The view was keyed to the body, so that lens was handed a dry frame: **3.75%** of it over 200/255, brightest pixel **255**, while **0 of 56,100** rays cast from it reached the sky — every one hit water first. The frame is now tinted by the water it is really looking through: mean luminance **142 -> 93.7**, blue-minus-red **+33.9 -> +111.5**, brightest pixel **255 -> 168**, over 200/255 **3.45% -> 0%** -- the water's own law switched on for the frame (`hazeOn 1`, `hazeK [1.40, 0.40, 0.15]`) and the water's own veil colour `[0.06, 0.24, 0.32]` taken outright instead of mixed 85% into the sky's. The body still owns breath, wetness and bubbles: in that same frame the head above the surface stays dry and the air still fills (air left above **5**). A level view, lens above the surface, is untouched -- the air's own `fogDen 0.0075` before and after, and the frame keeps its sky: **4,132** of those same 56,100 rays reach it. The haze belongs to the block now: `fogC / fogT / fogEmis / fogK` on the water row of the block table, read through `liquidFogAt()` -- the environment code used to carry a second, unlinked copy of the water's colour. A liquid that states a per-colour law (`fogK`) replaces the air's squared-exponential haze outright, and the air's own density is left alone; a liquid that states only a density keeps the air's shape and takes the density instead. So a second liquid -- a swamp, a lava -- would say all four of those things about itself and the environment code would not be edited at all; the one thing it would have to say that is baked in is its own absorption, which is measured into the world's vertices when they are built, and so would have to be baked alongside the depth rather than read off the frame.
- **The body is wet in the half that is under the water**: the body being filmed used to be the last thing drawn, after the water pass, so it rode on top of the lake's own blend and stayed dry from its feet to its head. Standing in a one-block shore shelf with the lens 5.55 m out over open water and 2.1 m above the surface, switching the water meshes off moved the lakebed behind the body by **148** on average, while the **642** pixels of the body below the surface line moved by **17** and only **13.2%** of them moved at all. The body now rides with the other bodies, before the glass and the water: **100%** of those pixels move, by **32.3** on average, and the half of the body under the surface reads blue over red by **+12.9** where the dry half of the same body in the same frame reads **-39**. The half above the surface line is untouched — **0** pixels changed, before and after — because the water writes no depth and cannot reach a fragment the body already drew in front of it. It tints the body without hiding it: **99.4%** of the body's silhouette still reads against the frame behind it.
- **One camera per frame**: `update()` shoots it once (`_camFrame`) and the picking ray, the highlight box, the interaction prompt and `render()` all read that same frame. The ray starts at the lens in third person and at the eye in first, but reach (5.5 m) is always measured from the body, never from the lens.
- **The opening frame belongs to the map**: `generate()` decides where the game opens **and which way the lens is turned**, and hands it over as `N.spawn` with a `yaw` on it; `init()` uses that yaw instead of a constant. The old rule only tested the column the player lands in and the two beside it — it never tested the direction the camera is pointing at open. It accepted a spot half a block from the main cellar's dark brick box, and 91% of the first frame was a wall 0.6 m away at a mean luminance of 57/255 with no sky in it at all. Walking away is what made it look better a few seconds later. The rule now casts a fan of rays (8×10, 76° field, 1.6 aspect) in each of the four cardinal directions from every street column near the cellar door, and takes the nearest column where at least one direction has **≥8% sky and ≤15% of pixels closer than 2 m**; if the village ever gets dense enough that no column passes, it takes the least bad one (≥3% sky, ≤30%) rather than dropping the player through a wall. Lanternrow now opens on the lantern road, 6.0 blocks from the cellar door, looking west along it. One lantern post is skipped along the way: the spacing put a post at x = 136, which is the cellar door's own column, so the door opened onto a lamp. `node-smoke.js` checks the spawn column is not buried and that the sight line is clear for 3 blocks — but that check passed on the old spot too, because its sight line went straight through the open doorway; only the frame check in `play.js` (the **whole** frame, not its upper half) sees a face pressed against a wall.

Measured in the prototype (1280×720, SwiftShader software rendering): all 100 chunks built, about 200–300k triangles, 47–400 draw calls, **54–60 fps**.

### 3.3 Data and saving

- Voxels: `Uint8Array(w*h*d)` = 160×48×160 = 1.23 MB.
- **Heightmap**: planned (`Uint8Array(w*d)`), not in the prototype. Bounding the meshing loop with one measured no faster, and it has to be right on every block write or geometry gets quietly clipped. Meshing scans the full chunk height; the time goes into the faces, not into the empty sky -- and the water table above is what proved it: the empty sky was being walked a second time, five map lookups for every vertex, and taking that out left **0.97 ms** for a chunk rebuild (**4.15 ms** before), about **0.09 ms per thousand vertices**.
- Save: `localStorage['frutaria.save.v3']`, storing only the **block diff** (`diffs`) + player (position, vitality, current form and its remaining time) + **identity** (`id` / `name`, see 03 §6) + backpack + storages (every field stash records the `owner` id that planted it) + regrow queue (`regrow`) + planting queue (`growQueue`) + fruit scattered by death (`drops`) + plant buffs + owl post + backpack upgrades + quests + time. Measured 2.3 KB with a cellar tree, a sapling and a post in flight. It is written by the Save button and by `Ctrl+S`, and the page writes it **on its way out** (`pagehide`) and **when it is put to sleep** (`visibilitychange`): between one save and the next the valley exists only in that tab, so a reload, a closed tab or a phone switched to another app must not cost the player everything since the last save. Both hooks are gated on the game having started — a page still sitting on the start screen is holding a valley it has not loaded, and writing that fresh world would overwrite the save the screen is offering back. `F5` stays bound for the browsers that hand that key to the page; the ones that keep it for themselves lose nothing either, because the page has already written the valley as it goes.
- A save is **offered back, never silently loaded**: the start screen quotes it (day, hour, pack, trees) and Continue walks you back to it. The town cellar belongs to the valley rather than to the save, so it is rebuilt from the map data and every store is looked up by `id` — a save that lists only cellar trees still has a town to walk into.

### 3.4 Interface language

The interface is switchable between Chinese and English at run time (`L`, or the button in the HUD); the choice is stored in `localStorage['frutaria.locale.v1']` and, on a first visit, taken from the browser language.

Two kinds of text exist and they are translated differently, on purpose:

| Kind | Where it lives | Looked up with | Examples |
|---|---|---|---|
| **interface text** | `data/i18n.js`, one flat key → string table per language | `N.t('hud.cap', {u, c})` | buttons, panels, prompts, log lines |
| **setting text** | on the record itself, in `data/fruits.js` / `creatures.js` / `world.js` / `player.js`, as a `cn` / `en` pair | `N.disp(record)` | a fruit's name and lore, a creature's dex entry, a zone name, an epithet |

Setting text stays next to the thing it describes because those data files are the source of truth the documents are checked against (`docs-sync.js` §7 compares the prose in `docs/cn/` and `docs/en/` with them). If the translations were moved into the language tables, the documents could drift away from the setting unnoticed.

Two consequences worth knowing:

- **Nothing translated is ever stored.** A store has no name of its own — the interface calls it by kind (`store.cellar` / `store.tree`) — and a quest keeps its text next to the check that completes it. So a save written in Chinese loads unchanged in English, and switching language cannot freeze a string into a warehouse. `game/test/lang.js` asserts the save file is byte-identical across languages.
- **Adding a language is one table.** `data/i18n.js` holds `LANGS` plus one object per language; `node-smoke.js` fails the build if the tables do not cover exactly the same keys.

### 3.5 Known technical debt

| Item | Note |
|---|---|
| No occlusion culling | blocks outside are still drawn while you are inside a cave |
| No async meshing | chunk meshing is budgeted at 22 ms per frame during boot; a player edit flags its chunk and the flagged chunks are re-meshed on a 6 ms per frame budget -- and the most expensive chunk in the valley rebuilds in **1.8 ms**, so one harvest never spends the whole budget (**22.5 ms** for the two chunks a pick flagged before the water table was built up front) |
| Creatures have no pathfinding | only "steer toward target + height correction + random deflection on hitting a wall", so they get stuck on terrain |
| No LOD / no streaming | the 160³ world is fully resident in memory; the full scale needs chunk streaming |
| No NPC dialogue trees | Old Mei / One-Eye only have log text, no branching dialogue |
| Cellar trees are never destroyed | the bear tear-down roll is not wired up (raids and owl post are implemented) |
| The frog does not dry out on screen | `dryTimer` is in the data and the check is wired up, but there is no skin-moisture bar in the HUD (04 §3) |
| No identity service | the `id` is generated locally, not issued by a server; cellar trees already carry an `owner`, but the main cellar is communal and there is no sharing-rights model (see 03 §6) |
| Only two languages | a third language means one more flat table in `data/i18n.js` plus a third field on every record that carries setting text (`cn` / `en` pairs in the data files) |
| The new hazards are invisible | the ridge cold is a rule the code runs, but there is no frost, no breath fog and no HUD cue — the only thing that tells you is a log line, and a player can die of it without ever seeing why; the owl's daylight glare is nothing but one more layer of fog |
| Zone prose is never displayed | every zone in `data/world.js` carries a `desc`, and no interface reads it. The map bible in 02 is the only place that writing reaches a reader, and it is Chinese-only, so it is not covered by the language switch either |
