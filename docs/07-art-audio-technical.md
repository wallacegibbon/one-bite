# 07 · Art · Audio · Technical Plan

## 1. Art direction

**Voxel, but not Minecraft.** References: the block language of *Minecraft* + the saturated palette of *Trove* + Ghibli's "plants have intentions".

Three hard rules:

1. **Every plant has something it "wants to do"**: fruit self-glows (wants to be seen), vines grow down (looking for water), mushrooms ring up (claiming ground).
2. **Buildings are grown, not laid**: Lanternrow's houses are timber frames with mossy-soil infill; Starfall Keep is brick plus lumenstone lamps; the Old Orchard is a ring of broken wall around the oldest tree in the valley.
3. **There are only three light sources**: sun/moon, lumenstone and embers (self-lit blocks), fruit (a fruit block lights itself, and so does one you carry), and one animal (the lanternfish parts are built emissive). No body you can wear is **born** with a light, and nothing you pick lends one: the light in the valley is the sun and the moon, the lumenstone and the embers, the fruit, the ring of the Glowglade and the lanternfish. **There is no ambient fill light** — at night, away from the lamps and the ring, you genuinely cannot see.

### Face lighting (baked, not real-time)

Each face gets a fixed brightness by normal, then vertex AO is multiplied in:

| Face | Brightness |
|---|---|
| +Y (top) | 1.00 |
| +Z | 0.88 |
| +X | 0.78 |
| −X | 0.66 |
| −Z | 0.58 |
| −Y (bottom) | 0.42 |

AO has four steps: `[1.00, 0.78, 0.60, 0.46]`, computed from each vertex's three neighbours (edge, edge, corner).

That table is for blocks with hard edges. A block may declare `soft: 1` instead, and then it keeps the same ordering of faces but never drops below **0.74** and takes no AO at all (`SOFT_S = 0.55 + 0.45·s`). Cloud (16) is the one that needed it: it was a block, so it went through the face table and the ambient occlusion of the cloud deck around it, and a bottom face in the shade of the block above measured 0.42 × 0.46 = 0.19 of its own colour. Cloud is 94% white, so the result was a dark grey lump floating in a bright sky — a black block in the sky, which is what it looked like. Measured in the frame while the deck stood: a cloud averaged **113** against the sky's 92; with the flag removed it averaged **70**, darker than the sky behind it. The deck itself is gone now — the sky has no cloud, the cut the user asked for — but the block stays: the Grey Veil wall is built out of it, so the veil reads as pale mist columns rather than as stone.

A canopy takes the same flag, for the same reason. A crown is a blob of leaf blocks; its underside is the face you look at when you stand under the tree, and the ring of neighbours the crevice test samples under it **is** the crown -- the dark ring under a tree is the tree. So the five canopy blocks (leaves 7, needles 8, silver leaves 9, dark leaves 27, willow leaves 28) are `soft: 1` too.

That still left the crevice test itself, which asks whether a face sits in a crevice and had been read as a brightness rule. A face looking out at open sky is by definition not in one, yet the underside of a crown scored the darkest step, because the solid all around it is the same crown. So every face is now also asked how far it looks along its own normal before it hits anything: `OPEN_R = 4` cells of clear air and it takes no crevice darkening at all; anything solid within two cells and it keeps all of it -- that is the contact shadow a cliff, a roof rim and a cave lip need; in between, the amount is interpolated. A bottom face also lifts toward the soft bottom shade, because the reason the table makes a bottom face 0.42 -- nobody looks at one -- is not true of a face hanging in open air.

Measured over the whole map, counting only the faces that have eight cells of clear air along their normal -- the faces a player sees against the sky, 107,880 of them: **60,506** rendered under 60/255, 56% of them, mean 73, worst 10. The exposure term alone takes that to 19,277 (18%); with the canopy flag as well, **15,421 (14%)**, mean 93, worst 39. What is left is the face table doing what it is told: a −Z face of a dark block is dark, and that is the fake sun, not a hole. Widening the ray to the widest of five rays was measured and changed nothing, so the ray stays straight. The extra ray costs nothing measurable: a full re-mesh of 100 chunks is ~100 ms before and after. In the rendered frame, over 216 views across the map, pixels darker than 55 with sky brighter than 130 within five pixels of them fell from **1.46%** of the frame to **0.68%**. The same measure at the two low-sun hours (07:00, 17:00) is **0.53%**, so noon is the worst case rather than the luckiest one. `game/test/play.js` reads the frame at the two places a player actually stands under this -- under a conifer crown, and under the town roof over the lantern road: a hanging face may be darker than the sky, but a quarter of its pixels must reach **45**/255 and the darkest twentieth must be past **35**. With the shading reverted those read 31 and 24 for the crown, 22 and 19 for the roof.

### Creature models

Assembled from voxel parts: one mesh + one pivot per part. A part may name a `parent`, so a limb has a joint -- shoulder to elbow, hip to knee. Parts are authored in the bind pose.

**Every model faces +Z**, and a heading of `yaw` in this game means the direction `(sin yaw, -cos yaw)`: yaw 0 is north (-Z) and yaw grows toward +X. The rotation that carries +Z onto a heading is therefore `PI - yaw`, and it exists in exactly one place, `M4.faceY`. Writing `yaw + PI` -- which all three call sites used to -- is exact only when heading due north or due south, and mirrors the body about the Z axis everywhere else: seen from behind, a body walking east showed its face, in first person the hand pointed sideways, and every animal in the valley walked backwards whenever it headed east or west. The `camera` step's angle checks cannot see it, because a mirrored model has the same angles, so `game/test/play.js` reads the frame: it stands the body at each of the four cardinal headings with the lens behind it, projects the head to the screen, and demands the back of the head -- hair, not a face -- at every one of them.

| Animation | Used for | Formula |
|---|---|---|
| `walk` | thigh / upper arm | `rx = −asin(sin(0.9·stride·amp)·footAt(u))` — the angle that puts the foot where it belongs along its own stride. `u = gait + (sign < 0 ? 0.5 : 0)` is where the body is in its own stride, one whole cycle per stride of ground, the two sides of a body half a stride apart, and the caller advances it by the ground the body covered and never by a clock. `stride` is the part's own share of the swing: a frog's legs take none of it, because a frog does not walk on land, it hops |
| `knee` | shin | a knee folds one way only, and only while the foot is off the ground: `rx = bend·0.62·stride·fold(u)·move`, where `fold(u)` is nothing at all through the stance and one hump through the swing. When it folds is said by the stride itself and not by a quarter cycle of a clock: a model with no ankle has nowhere for a folded planted knee to go but down. `bend` says which way this model's knee folds (absent = the human's way) and the `sign` mirror is deliberately **not** applied to the fold |
| `elbow` | forearm | the same fold on an arm, smaller, with a little of it always held, **and the arm held the more bent the faster the body goes**: `rx = bend·(0.12+0.3·fold(u)·move+0.55·move²)` — an arm hanging dead straight is a stick, and a straight arm swung through 2.3 strides a second is a pendulum the body has to stop and start again twice a stride. A body standing still holds **0.12 rad** as it always did, a body walking still lets its arm hang (**0.14**), and a body running carries it bent (**0.61** held all the way through the stride, **0.89** at the widest moment of the swing). An arm swings opposite the leg on the same side, which is what the half stride `sign` buys it |
| `twist` | torso | `ry = footAt(u)·move·0.16` — the part above the legs turning against them, on the stride and not on the clock, or the shoulders and the feet are two unrelated things at any speed other than the one the clock happens to agree with |
| `nod` | head | `rx = sin(t·1.9)·(move·0.06+0.02)` |
| `wag` | tail, and the body built to sway with it | `ry = sin(t·1.6 + wagPhase)·(move·wagAmp + wagIdle)` — the tail and the fins keep the old kick whole (`wagAmp` 0.5, `wagIdle` 0.12, no phase), and a body that kicks its tail but holds its heading straight reads as sliding: the tail goes left and right and the body goes straight, which is a body being dragged. So the fish's own body carries the same kick on the same clock, smaller — the body is the part that is mostly going straight and the tail is the part that swings — and a quarter beat **ahead** of the tail, because the kick travels from the body out to the tail, and a wave that runs tail-to-body is a fish swimming backwards (`wagAmp` 0.16, `wagIdle` 0.04, `wagPhase` = π/2). Measured off the model's own angles at the body's own cruise speed: the body swings **±0.2 rad** and the tail **±0.62**, and the body's peak comes **0.98 s** — a quarter of the kick's 3.9 s period — before the tail's |
| `bob` | body | the heave is the stride: `dy = rideOf(stance)·cos((u−stance/2)·4π)·move·0.20 + sin(t·2.2)·0.05` — twice a stride, because the body stands now on one leg and now on the other. **Which way round it goes is decided by whether the stride has a flight phase in it.** While some foot is always down, the legs are **struts**: the body is highest over the middle of a stance, where the strut is straight and under it. Once a stance is under half a stride there is a moment in every stride with no foot down at all, and the leg is a **spring** instead — it comes down onto a foot, bends it, and is thrown back off it — so the body is at its **lowest** over the middle of a stance and highest in the middle of the flight. `rideOf(stance)` is that turnover: 1 at a stance of half a stride or more, −1 at `STANCE_RUN` (0.30), eased in between. A person at 4.7 m/s has a stance of 0.319, so the old heave — always highest at mid-stance — put the body at its top precisely when its leg was taking its weight. Measured off the angles the draw uploads, over one whole stride: a person at 4.7 m/s rises and falls **0.055 m** across a stride, lowest at **0.158** of it (mid-stance is 0.16) and highest at **0.408** (mid-flight is 0.41); at a walk, where the stance is 0.556 and there is no flight phase, the other way round — highest at **0.279** (mid-stance 0.278), lowest at **0.029** (the two feet change over at 0.028). And the heave is worth more ground the faster the body goes: **0.025 m** at 0.35 of the person's top speed against **0.055 m** at 0.94 of it. On the clock it was a sine of its own, at a rate nothing in the walk decided. The idle part stays on the clock, and a body with no leg that walks has no stride to ride either. A person's own body had no heave at all and now carries one, on its hips; the limbs it stands on cancel it, which is why the feet stay planted while the body above them rides |
| `pulse` | the frog's throat | a scale, not a rotation |

**A foot that has reached the ground stays there.** The walk cycle used to be one clock for every legged body -- the hip at `sin(t·7)`, the rate a constant -- while the body was driven along by its own speed, and the two had never been spoken to. Read off the matrices the draw uploads, a foot was travelling along the ground at **3.0 m/s** while its body went past at **4.1**: a foot was on the ground for four frames in five and held still in none of them, and the whole animal was skating on grass. It is not a tuning problem. A foot sits at the bottom of a sine's arc exactly where that sine is turning over fastest, so the fastest moment of a swing is the moment the foot has to be at its slowest, and no rate fixes that. So the cycle is written as the two acts it has, and the ground keeps the time. `footAt(u)` says where the foot is along its own stride: a straight line from +1 to −1 through the **stance**, because a foot on the ground is being carried along a flat surface by the body and a straight line is the only thing that keeps it there, and an eased curve back from −1 to +1 through the **swing**, which is the only part of a stride a knee may fold in. The share of a cycle that a stance takes is not a constant either -- **62%** at a walk, **30%** at the fastest a body goes -- and the rest of a fast stride is travelled through the air along with the body, which is where a long stride comes from without any leg being swung any wider. The hip angle is then whatever puts the foot at that place, and it is negative because a positive turn about x carries a foot hanging under it toward −z while every model here faces +z. How wide the swing is (`amp = move·stance/0.62`) is the knob that decides how a speed turns into steps, and the two obvious ways to turn it are both wrong: swing wider and a fast body takes long slow strides, keep the swing and shorten the stance and it takes long fast ones, and either way the rate of the steps **falls** as the body goes faster, which is the opposite of what a body does. What a body does is hold its step rate nearly the same and change the length of a step -- a person walking at 1 m/s takes steps of 0.4 m at about 2.5 a second, and running at 4.7 m/s takes steps of 1.9 m at about the same 2.5 -- and making the swing proportional to speed *times the stance* is what makes the two cancel in the one place they have to: a stride comes out proportional to the speed, and the rate of the steps comes out a property of the leg alone. What one cycle is worth along the ground is not a constant either: it is measured, once per model, as how far that model's own sole reaches fore and aft under the body, through the same matrices the draw uploads -- the hinge that sits lowest is not the one the foot hangs from, and the lift that keeps a foot out of the ground moves the whole limb -- and the caller advances the phase by the ground the body covered, so one cycle carries the body exactly that far. Measured over a flat stretch walked at the speed the body walks at: **1.85 m** of stride for a person at 4.7 m/s against the model's own **1.85**; **2.33** steps a second. And the foot holds: over the frames in which the model says a foot is on the ground, that foot travels **0.006** of the ground the body covers in those same frames -- the remainder is a foot with no ankle, rocking from toe to heel as the leg sweeps under the body -- where before it was over **1**. A body standing still has no stride at all: its legs stop, its feet move **0 m** in two seconds, and a body walked into a wall stops its legs with its body instead of running on the spot. 
A part may also declare `air:[rx,ry,rz]` and `crouch:[rx,ry,rz]`: the pose it takes when the body is off the ground, and when its legs are loaded. The weights come from the physics, not from a guess about which animal is being drawn -- `air` is how far off the ground the body actually is, `crouch` how long its feet have been down since the last launch, and `squash` the speed it arrived at the ground with, applied to the whole body as a scale. A body that does not hop is never animated as if it did. A limb the body stands on says so with `stands: 1`, and its off-ground pose then comes only as far as the body is actually off the ground -- `off`, the same 0..1 clearance the caller measures in body heights. Not for tidiness: a hind leg built longer than the body is tall sweeps its foot clean through the plane it stands on on the way into the pose and on the way out of it, because the pose is eased and the body's own origin does not move with it. Read off the draw: a frog landing from a hop drove its shin **0.154 m** into the ground it had just touched, in **169** frames of **360**, with the pose nowhere near the ground at the time.

A part may also declare `swimKick: 1` (this part drives the kick when the body is swimming), `heldPose: [rx,ry,rz]` (the pose it is held in while the water is holding the body up) and `swimPose: [rx,ry,rz]` (the pose it is held in while swimming). `swim` and `held` are the fourth and fifth signals the caller passes. `swim` is a **ratio** rather than a flag -- how near the speed its own record swims at the body is actually moving, and 0 for a body the data gives no swim speed to: **a human in the lake wades**, because the human model has no swim parts and the game has no human stroke. It used to be floored at 0.18, so that a body coasting to a stop kept paddling instead of winding down, and that floor is where the complaint about a hovering frog came from: a body the water held up with nowhere to go walked its feet **1.55 m** through four seconds of standing still. The ratio is handed in by the caller now (`strokeOf`, in the game loop) and it is sized by the water the body is actually pushing, and under the stroke the body is pushing with sits a floor the size of the work of holding still (`TREAD` = 0.18 of the stroke): a body that is going nowhere has no stroke of going, but the water is holding it up and something has to do the work, so its legs do the same table shrunken to the effort of staying in place, on the treads' own clock with the stroke's hold taken out (the treads, below), and the body keeps its small ride with the drive. `held` is the water holding the body up -- in water, breathing it, and not standing on the bottom -- the same reading the physics uses to decide whether a body floats (03 §4), and it is what folds the knee and puts the feet **behind** the body instead of under it, and rolls the whole leg out **away from the centre line** with it. The fold alone leaves a leg bolted to the flank: read off those same matrices, a shin stood **0.063 m** out past the flank of a body **0.169 m** half-wide, with the centre of its foot **0.152 m** off the centre line -- a frog seen from behind was a pair of trousers dropped over the water. Rolled in, the leg went the other way instead: a foot reached **0.115 m** past the centre line of its own body floating and **0.119 m** swimming, both feet pointed in, and the leg came only **0.024 m** and **0.052 m** past the flank -- a frog seen from behind was a pair of legs crossed under itself. Rolled out by 0.1 rad at the hip and 0.1 at the knee, the centre of a foot sits **0.234 m** out on its own side floating and **0.272 m** swimming, no foot ever reaches the centre line (the worst the whole stroke manages is 0.001 m), and the leg stands **0.147 m** and **0.176 m** past the flank; that turn is split over both joints rather than hung on the knee, because the **0.5 rad** the fold used to be given at the knee alone throws the whole leg **0.218 m** past the flank, and a frog floating against the wall of the lake has its feet in the rock. On land the legs stay out at the sides, as the haunch of a frog does: **0.078 m** and **0.181 m**, and that is the pose the water pose is measured against -- the water legs reach more than twice as far out as the land haunch does, which is what a webbed foot is for; the widest they are ever carried is at a slow stroke, where a wild frog crossing the lake takes a leg **0.192 m** past the flank. A body standing in water, dug down on the lakebed or wading where the lake is one block deep, has feet to stand on and stops kicking: a stroke there is a body kicking at nothing. A body the water holds up and that is going nowhere treads: its foot moves **0.079 m** fore and aft and **0.01 m** up and down in the same three seconds the standstill used to move it **0 m**. The treads are not the stroke slowed down: the stroke carries a hold — the last **44%** of the cycle the legs shut behind the body and do nothing — and a body that treads to stay afloat does not stop its legs between the strokes, because the water is holding it up the whole time and something has to keep doing the work the whole time: a leg that reached back, froze, sat still, and started again is a leg that is not treading, and from the bank the body read as frozen in the middle of the lake — the one thing a body the water is holding up is not. So the treads run the same table the stroke reads, on their own slower clock (**2.1 rad/s**, one slow pump every **3 s**) with the hold taken out — a continuous pump between the gather, where the feet are drawn up beside the hips, and the drive, where they shoot back — and the full stroke takes over over the first third of the way to the body's own speed, so the legs never stop and start again as the body gets up to speed: they just get faster. A frog does not run on the surface, and it does not paddle a sine either. Both hind legs stroke **together**, one stroke every **1.5 s** (the stroke's own clock is `SWIM_RATE` = 4.2 rad/s) rather than the walk cycle's 7 strides a second, and the stroke is a frog's breaststroke: four keys the joints are eased between -- **gliding**, the pair shut behind the body and the legs run out (knee at **140 deg**, the two feet **0.47 m** apart); **gathered**, the knees drawn apart and the shins folded up against the thighs, so each webbed foot is drawn up beside its own hip, under the rear of the body and never under its middle (knee down to **52 deg**, the pair **0.54 m** across); **driven**, the knees extending and the feet shooting back and in together (**0.17 m** apart, knee run out to **175 deg**, short of straight); and back to the glide, which is what the stroke is for. The angles are offsets from the pose the water already holds the leg in, so a stroke starts and ends where a floating leg already sits. The old cycle was one sine per joint: it ran a foot **0.43 m** fore and aft while the pair stayed **0.43 m** apart through the whole of it -- two legs cycling up and down under a body, shutting no water anywhere -- and seen from behind the animal never changed shape. The front legs fold along the body. The `sign` mirror is dropped for the stroke's timing on purpose -- a synchronous pair is not mirrored in time, and the model data already mirrors the geometry -- but the stroke's sideways turn keeps it, taken from each part's own side, so the whole stroke is one table read twice and the two sides cannot drift out of step with each other the way hand-mirrored data does. Measured on the angles the renderer uploads: on land the two hind thighs sit 1.24 / 1.50 / 1.31 rad apart from each other (alternating), in the water 0.000 (together), and the front legs go from alternating to held at 0.55 rad. Measured in the frame: the silhouette seen from directly behind agrees with its own mirror at **0.965** and **0.957** at the two extremes of one stroke, while those two extremes agree with each other at only **0.559** -- the legs really do move between them. The frame bound is the loose half of the pair -- from directly behind, a leg forward and a leg back move 6% of the outline -- so the claim is also read off the angles themselves, which is where it is actually made. And the foot is followed through the two joint matrices the draw uploads, on the frame a swimming body travels fastest on: its centre runs from **0.26 m behind** the body's rear edge to **0.07 m in front of** it -- still **0.10 m** short of the middle of the body, and never under it -- **0.33 m** of sweep, and it rises **0.19 m**: the gathered foot is the high one, come up to **0.22 m** above the model's own origin, which is the height its own hip hangs it at (**0.23 m**) and well below the back of the body (**0.33 m**), and the driven foot is the low one at **0.04 m**. The knee folds to **52 deg** and runs back out to **175 deg**, never past straight. A wild frog crossing the lake at its own cruising speed sweeps **0.18 m** and keeps its foot **0.17 m** behind that edge at the far end; a body floating with nowhere to go sweeps **0.08 m** of the same stroke and leaves its feet at the edge; and a body standing in the water keeps the pose it has on dry ground, its foot **0.18 m** in front of the edge either way, the two poses agreeing to within 0.002 m. A turn is kicked with as well as leaned into: the caller hands in `turn` as a **signed** rad/s of heading, and the stroke's sideways turn is scaled by it, so the leg on the outside of the turn takes the longer bite (`TURN_KICK` = 0.7, full at `TURN_FULL` = 0.9 rad/s of heading) and the leg on the inside the shorter one -- a body turning right drives with the leg on its own left, a body turning left with the one on its right. Only the stroke's outward half is scaled (`TURN_FOLD` = 0.3 on the hip's pitch column); the shut is left to the pose, because both feet finish together on the centre line behind the body, and scaling the shut as well drags the outside foot **0.068 m** across it. Read off the matrices the draw uploads, at the frog's own full turn rate: the outside leg sweeps **0.37 m** and carries its foot **1.02 m** through the water, the inside only **0.29 m** and **0.72 m** -- the outside leg sweeps 1.41 times what the inside one does -- and it stands **0.04 m** further out than the inside leg does; its knee still folds to **35 deg**, and its foot still stays behind the body and never under its middle (**0.25 m** of rise against **0.37 m** of sweep). A body steered straight has `turn` exactly 0 and strokes alike on both sides; a body turning left and a body turning right give the same numbers with the two legs exchanged. And that is the only door a heading rate has into a pose at all: `partAngles` reads `turn` only inside the stroke branch, so the same 1.9 rad/s handed to a walking person or a hopping frog moves their legs **0 rad** -- filmed with it left in and with it pinned out, the frames differ by **0** of 504000 pixels -- while handed to a frog stroking water it swings its thigh **0.179 rad**.

**The body itself has two pose signals: `bank` (lean into the turn) and `tilt` (nose along the path).** A body carried by water leans into its turn and points along the course it is actually taking; a body on foot does neither, because feet grip and the ground answers. Neither is a part animation — the renderer rotates the whole body, first about its own facing axis, then about its own lateral axis — and both are one rule and one set of numbers for the player's body and for every animal's. The lean is the sideways push the arc needs over the pull the body works against: `atan(turn rate × speed / 9.8)`, clamped to 0.45 rad, eased at 4.5. The game's own gravity is 23 m/s² because a jump has to read, and it is deliberately *not* the number here, because a lean is not a fall. The pitch is the same idea turned sideways: `atan(−climb rate / speed)`, clamped to 0.6, eased at 4.5, nose-down positive, the climb rate being the height the body just gained or lost. Measured: a body swimming a fixed course peaks at **0.324 rad** (18.6°); a body on foot driven through the same quarter turn stays at **0**; a body letting go of its height settles at **0.542** nose-down, a body climbing at **−0.31**, a body on foot at **0**; and the draw reads the lean, the same body level and banked 0.42 rad differing by **10130** pixels of frame between the two draws. There was one exemption, and it is gone with the body it was written for: a bell that pulses has no nose to point, so the lamp jellyfish leaned and never pitched -- the lanternfish that took its place has a nose, and it pitches with the rest of the valley. The turn rate handed to the lean is **signed**, and it used not to be: the lean was built from the absolute value of the heading change, so every body banked the same way whichever way it turned -- a body turning left leaned into the turn and a body turning right leaned out of it, and the two legs' kicks had nothing honest to be read against. That rate carries a dead band of **0.12 rad/s** as well: a heading read twice a frame off a body crawling through water and brushing its banks jitters by a degree or two, and a lean built on that jitter weaves the shoulders of a body swimming dead straight. Measured: a body steered straight now holds `turn` at exactly 0 across all **94** frames of its run, and a body driven through a quarter turn peaks at **1.46 rad/s** with its two legs' kicks mirrored to within **0.02**.

**Nothing with a body turns on the spot.** A body the water is holding bends the course it is already on, at `turn` radians a second — a field on the animal record and the same number on the form record of the same species, because the animal and the body worn from it are one body: frog 0.9, lanternfish 0.8, human 1.6. Above its own cruising speed the arc gets wider rather than the turn getting faster, because the same push has more body to swing about; and the rate is capped as well at 2.2 × how far off course the body is, which is what stops a body weaving when the thing it is aimed at and the way it is going disagree. Measured before: a frogpear in the water opened a turn at **94 rad/s** and crossed the lake at **9.9 m/s**, against the 4.6 m/s its own record swims at. After: peak **1.90 rad/s** and **4.63 m/s** held to within 0.04 of the record the whole way round a circle. The limit itself was then lowered from 1.9 to 0.9, with the record that measured it: every errand a frog picks lies somewhere off its nose, and a correction wider than half a turn at 1.9 is 109 degrees a second, which from the bank reads as a head swung at the end of a body. The small corrections run against the 2.2 × how-far-off-course cap and are unchanged; only the wide ones came down, from a whip to a glide. Measured after: a wild frog in the lake comes about 90 degrees in **1.75 s** at a peak of **0.90 rad/s** and holds an arc **2.1 m** wide at its wander speed, and the body worn from it crosses the same quarter turn in **0.88 s** at the same peak. The course really is an arc: the frog's path measures **3.16 m** along and **2.98 m** straight across, while a body steered straight measures 3.16 along and 3.16 across. A body on foot is deliberately *not* arc-limited: feet grip, and a body on foot turning is the answer to the mouse, not to a rudder.

**An animal that cannot make the turn slows for it.** The arc of a turn is speed over rate, so a place nearer than the arc, or off to one side, is a place the animal circles and never arrives at. The speed that puts the circle through the place comes from the geometry: the way to the place is a chord of the circle, and a chord that meets the heading at the course error belongs to a circle whose radius is half the distance over the sine of that error. The naive bound — the distance over the sine, without the half — is exactly the speed at which the animal circles for ever at its own arc width: a frog sent to a place 1.2 m to one side walked **48 m** round it. Measured on the same two errands after: the frog stands **0.79 m** from that place having walked **1 m**, and a lanternfish **0.8 m** from one 0.9 m off having walked **0.4 m** (before: 23 m round it). Out in the open, where the place is far and the sine is small, the rule costs nothing; near it, it reads as an animal easing as it comes about, which is what it is.

**An animal that meets a wall goes round it.** Of the four steps it can take it takes the one that stands and points most the way it wants to go, and the heading follows the step at the animal's own turn rate — the contour of the obstacle rather than a fresh errand. Giving up was the whole of the old behaviour: in front of a cliff the step was rejected, the errand dropped, and the same rock walked into again. After: the frogs, which meet the same walls all along the lakeshore, walk in **99%** of the frames of a ninety-second night watch (**169 m** of it), and the lamp field keeps drifting round the lake. The lamp meets the same rule in the middle of its own field — the field is the open water of the lake, a disc of 38 m round the centre of the water, and its wall is the brick of the sunken tower, 17 m off that centre: the errand is picked wet in the disc, often on the far side of the brick, and the contour of the brick toward the errand is the same contour — the step that closes on the errand most, and the heading follows the step — with the eight ways the water branch has always had, because the lamp's other obstacle in the open water is a neighbour's trunk and the shortest way out of one is very often a corner of the grid. What the homeward slide it used to take instead kept was the nose: the heading was never told to follow it, so a lamp whose errand lay behind the wall kept its nose on the brick and, when the wall gave it no way, set its errand to its own feet and waited, **322 to 422** times a watch while the field was the ring on the tower's outline and the brick stood in the middle of it -- **20** a watch when the contour started running toward the errand, **7** a watch now that the brick is 17 m off the centre of a field the school swims the whole of. The contour toward the errand ends where the brick ends, because the errand is picked wet in the disc, and the field swims it harder as well: the median lamp covers **277 m** of the field in the five-minute watch. One turn budget a frame, whoever spent it: steering at the errand and stepping round the obstacle each bent the heading by the full allowance.

**Feet that are on a surface are standing on it.** The contact flag used to be lit only by a move the collision had to stop, and a body is parked 0.0005 m off the face it last hit, so a frame has to sink that far before the cell below is sampled again. Gravity covers it on land -- 0.006 m in a frame -- so the flag only flickers out for a frame at a time and the eased pose hides it. In water it cannot: buoyancy cancels most of the weight and the drag leaves a terminal sink of about 0.05 m/s, 0.0008 m a frame, so the bottom is sampled once every three frames. A body wading where the lake is one block deep -- and the shore of Mirrorlake is 183 such columns -- was then reported airborne the whole time it stood on the bottom: the air pose eased up to 0.42 and stayed there, and the steering kept a third of its push (accel 12 against 34). So after the displacement, feet within 2 cm of a solid and the body not rising means it is standing; 2 cm is the distance the step-up already uses to settle a body back onto the ledge it climbed, and far less than the fall a body picks up once it has left a surface (0.006 m in the first frame, 0.10 m by the fifth). Measured: standing in such a column the body is on the bottom **100%** of frames with the pose at **0** -- before, 50% and 0.42 -- and wading along the shelf it covers **5.19 m** in two seconds with its feet down; and the flag is not glue, a body that walks off a single step is off the ground for **18** of the next 40 frames, pose eased to **0.88**, having dropped **1.0 m**.

**A limb with no ankle lifts, and a limb that stands refuses to be carried down.** A thigh that swings, or a knee that folds, carries the corner of the foot that points the way of the swing down through the line the foot rests on. Nothing about the gait is wrong when that happens -- the model is simply missing a joint, and the limb has to make up for it by lifting. So every part that turns in the land gait, and whose limb reaches the ground line, carries the corners of the boxes it turns, its own and every part hanging under it (a thigh that swings carries the foot at the end of it), measured from its hinge; and it is lifted by as much as its own turn drives those corners below where they rest. The lift goes to the **top** of the limb, so the whole limb moves together and the joint between thigh and shin does not open. The other half is the heave: the body rides its own stride as it walks (`bob`, ±0.25 of the model's own units at its fastest -- 0.20 of stride and 0.05 of idle breath) and the limbs hang off it, so a body that dips takes its feet with it -- 6 cm into the floor, twice a stride, on every step. A limb standing on the ground does not go down when the body above it comes up, so it cancels that heave, and only the top of the limb cancels it, or the answer is counted twice. Only limbs that reach the ground line are given the table: a forearm swings, and drops, and nothing about it should move. The lift is one-way for a turn -- a foot may rise in mid-swing, it may not go under -- and two-way for the heave. Read off the draw over 180 frames of walking and 120 of standing on one flat clearing: a person's boot reached **0.032 m** under the floor in **32** frames of its walk, a frog's shin **0.164 m** in **169** of 360. After: **0.000 m** for the person, **0.007 m** for the frog. Which half closes which: the knee's quarter-cycle lag closes the frog, and the lift closes the person's boot (**0.033 m** with it switched off). And a lift must not carve a limb off the body it hangs from, so the same step measures the gap between a part and the part above it: every body sits at **0** against a 5 mm bound. A heave is bounded the same way, by how deep a leg is carved into the part it hangs from: a person's thigh boxes reached only 0.1 of their own units -- 1.8 cm -- into the hips, and a body that rides its stride by 0.25 units at that scale needs 4.5 cm of overlap, so they were carved to 0.3 units. The extra 2 cm sits inside the hips box, in the same colour, and is never seen.

A model carries **no scale of its own**. One number sizes a body: the collision box in `data/creatures.js` (`size`, in metres). The model is authored in unit boxes, and the creature's `scale` turns them into metres — so the model and the box are the same body twice, and if they disagree the player is clipped by geometry they can see themselves not touching. `game/test/node-smoke.js` measures every model against the box its creature declares and fails the build when it overhangs: the model's horizontal radius may exceed the box's half-width by at most 0.1 m, its top the box's height by at most 0.06 m. A **radius**, not a width, because the box is a square footprint that does not turn with the body — a model that fits face-on still sticks through a wall sideways. Before the gate existed the frog's snout reached 0.13 m past its own box.

The lanternfish is the one model with **no box to be measured against**: nothing in the game collides with it (you swim through the lamps), and it bears no fruit, so no player ever wears it. It is checked against the size the codex gives it instead — **0.64 m across, 0.24 m from the back fin to the belly**, measured at its `scale` of 0.16.

**A joint has to be lit, not only drawn.** A model is boxes that touch -- **49.0%** of a frog's box corners stand against another box, **45.8%** of a person's -- and every part used to be built with one flat shade per face, so a leg meeting a body was a notch in the outline and nothing more: measured over the pixels of a frog filmed the way the game films it -- the player's own eye, 76 degrees, one body alone in the frame, and the body's own idle phase pinned -- **98.1%** of the neighbouring pixel pairs inside the animal were exactly the same colour, and a frog's leg and body are the same green to begin with: with the crease switched off at the model **0** of the **336** faces of the two bodies carry a gradient at all, and the mesh of every body holds **6** distinct shade bytes, which is six face constants and nothing else. Each corner of each box is now asked the ambient-occlusion question the voxel world asks, of the model's own boxes and continuously -- there is no grid to look up: is there another box with volume beyond the plane of this face that reaches past the corner along one axis of the face while covering it along the other (side 1), the same along the other axis (side 2), and reaches past along both (corner)? Each of the three takes the nearest such box, graded by how far it stands (`CREASE_REACH` = 0.25 of the model's own typical box), and the terrain's clamp is kept: with both sides shut, the diagonal is not counted a second time. A box **flush** with the face has no volume beyond its plane and gets no say, which is what keeps a body built out of coplanar plates -- a frog's back plates, a person's chest and shoulder line -- from growing a dark grid across itself: only an inset or an overhang closes a corner. The depth is capped far short of the terrain's (`CREASE_DEPTH` = 0.35 of the face shade, against a crevice table that goes down to 0.46), because the reason a voxel face is dark -- it sits in a crevice, under something, sees no sky -- is not true of a knee: the deepest a corner can be shut is 1 - 0.35 x 5/6 = **0.71** of the face it stands on. It is computed once, from the bind pose, because a crease belongs to the joint: a leg swinging mid-stride carries the shading it had standing still. Emissive parts take none -- a lamp lights itself. Measured after, over the four bearings each body was filmed from (the flank on each side, the face and the back) with the palette held and only the crease switched: the flat fraction of that frog falls from **98.1%** to **87.8%**, and the distinct colours inside it from **44.5** to **373.5** on average; a person **91.1%** to **80.8%** and **36.5** to **126.5**; the meshes of a model now hold **38** distinct shade bytes for a person and **20** for a frog, where every model used to hold **6**. The parts that stay flat are the parts that should: a shin is a column the same width from hip to foot, and a column of constant width has no joint to show. `game/test/play.js` reads the shade bytes back off the meshes as they are uploaded and asks them corner by corner -- **14** checks, each one tied to the vertex's own position, so a crease that landed on the wrong corner cannot pass -- and with the crease reverted **8** of the **14** go red.

**A joint the lens sees has to be two colours, not one colour at two strengths.** The renderer multiplies a surface's colour and does nothing else to it (`vCol = aCol.rgb * aData.x * lit * dayLight + uAmb`): the face constant, the crease byte and the daylight are each one number, and the ambient term is `[0,0,0]` at the hour the tests freeze the clock at. Two boxes painted with colours of the **same ratios** therefore leave the frame the same colour whenever those scalars happen to even out, and a thigh behind a back, a shin under a haunch are precisely where they do. Only a difference in the ratios can tell such a joint apart, and the ratios are the palette, not the light. So the palette is measured at the joints, in the frame, with the parts told apart by an id buffer built out of an ordinary frame: every box of one part is repainted a marker colour of its own, and a pixel belongs to the part whose marker ray its colour sits on -- multiplication cannot move a colour off its own ray, so twelve markers standing at least 17.7 degrees apart are enough, and a pixel sitting on two rays or on none is nobody's. A seam is a pair of neighbouring pixels the buffer puts on two different parts, and what is read across it is the share each channel holds in its own pixel's luminance, because a scalar multiplication leaves every share alone: a difference in shares cannot have come from the light. The pose is pinned, because the draw clocks a body off the world's clock plus a phase the valley deals out at random (`game.js`: `t = this.time + c.phase`), and the seam between a tucked leg and the trunk it hops from is a near-tangency in the silhouette: two runs of a step that leaves that phase alone measured **98** pixels of that joint and then **4** pixels of it, with nothing changed in between. Pooled over the four bearings, before and after, the colour difference across the seam and the share of the seam that reads as two colours: the frog's skull on its back **0.085** and **23%** → **0.138** and **100%** over **165**; the frog's thigh behind its back **0.014** and **0%** → **0.126** and **100%** over **73**, and **7%** of that seam used to be the very same colour on both sides of the line, where now none of it is. Two colours moved: the frog's skull `#6a9a52` → `#68965c` and its thigh muscle `#5f8f4a` → `#6d8a4a`. And the flatness goes with it: pooled over the four bearings the commonest single colour in a frog's body used to cover **35.7%** of its pixels, now **30.6%** (**45.3%** → **31.9%** along one flank, **23.2%** → **16.2%** along the other); the longest unbroken run of one colour across the body did not move (**192**, **192**, **226**, **174** pixels, before and after alike) and is not claimed, because a run that long is one box's own back and repainting two boxes differently cannot break a run lying inside one box. What was left alone, and why: the person's hip and thigh are one pair of trousers on purpose (**0.003** and **0.009** across that seam) and every joint of theirs the frame does show already holds **0.10** to **0.79** of a colour difference -- coat against trousers **0.50**, satchel against coat **0.79**, bare skin against the coat **0.21** -- so nothing of that palette was moved, and at the shot the game films a person with (**5.55 m**, **84 degrees**) their body is only about **1500** pixels, too few for twelve markers to tell twelve parts apart over, which is why the claim is asked of the animals and not of the person; and the frog's head to its thigh was measured and is not claimed, because at those four bearings the two never stand against each other in the frame at all. The guards that keep such a measure from being vacuous ride in the test: the frame is read twice and must not move by one pixel, over nine tenths of an animal's own pixels must be attributed to a part (the person excepted, at seven tenths), the markers must sit **15** degrees apart, and every joint claimed must be worth **40** pixels of seam. With the two colours put back the palette checks go red: the idle reading of those same joints, and the reading of them on a body in motion. And the same joints are read a second time with the body actually moving, at the pose swept back out of the model's own `partAngles` rather than invented: the tuck a frog's legs only fold once its feet are clear of the floor it stands on (given the tuck with its feet on that floor the model moves nothing at all, **0** rad). A moving body loses joints rather than gaining them, so the two colours are asked of those frames as a separate question, and there the joint holds **0.110** of colour difference over **100%** of the seam. The pose is guarded twice over: it must move the model (**1.4** rad, the frog's tuck, at the pose the sweep picks), and it must reach the frame (a shin **121** rows off the floor it stood on, a thigh **48** rows up under the trunk it hops from).

**A marking has to arrive, not only be built.** One box was written into a model and never once reached a frame, because it stood wholly inside another box of the same part: the person's collar inside the skull it wraps. A part is drawn as one piece and turns as one piece, so no pose can ever dig such a box back out -- six faces uploaded for nothing, and a marking the setting promises the body has while the player never sees it. The geometry finds that case by itself (every corner of A inside B, B a box of the same part: the count across the two bodies is now **0**) and the frame answers the question that matters, by taking the box away -- the pixels that go with it are exactly what that box was worth. Each box is read twice, from the side its own most-open face points at and from over the body, and the better reading kept, because a marking that rises out of a body is buried on every side and escapes only upward. Measured with the shot the game films that body with (a person from 5.55 m at 84): the collar, rewritten as a ruff round the neck that reaches **0.395 m** out -- 8.5 cm past the 0.31 m collision footprint, inside the 0.10 m the model gate allows -- **333** pixels, **238** of them seen along the body, which is where a collar is supposed to show. The guards against a vacuous measure ride along: the person's own coat is worth **538** pixels and its skull **420** at the same shot, so a marking worth a few hundred is not being picked out of an empty frame.

### 3.2 Rendering

- **Chunks**: 16×48×16, 10×10 = 100 chunks. Each chunk has 6 meshes: `[opaque, emissive, water, props, prop-emissive, glass]`.
- **Face culling**: faces adjacent to a solid opaque neighbour are not generated.
- **A body is wound outward, and the culler has been on since the first commit**: `CULL_FACE` with `cullFace(BACK)` is enabled for every frame, and every face `MeshBuilder.box()` emitted was wound the other way -- so what a body presented to the lens was the inside of its far walls, one body-depth behind the flank you were standing in front of, for every creature and every micro box in the valley. The frame never looked broken, because an inside-out box still draws a closed silhouette. What it costs is depth: anything standing inside that depth shows through the animal in front of it. Measured with a probe box planted wholly inside a body, in a 640×480 frame: it is drawn either way, and only once the faces are wound outward is it hidden -- taking it away moves the pixels it covered with the winding as it was and **0** with it in. `node-smoke.js` now walks every triangle of all three models and fails the build unless the normal of each one points out of the box it belongs to: **816** triangles checked (the three models plus a probe box that exists only to be measured), all outward. The frame says it twice over: the end of a body the lens stands at reads as the end it was aimed at -- the head end of a flank about **1.6** times as bright as the taper end, **1.57** in one clearing and **1.72** in another, and at most **1.13** once every box is wound inward again, below the **1.3** the test holds -- and the crease step, which names the position of every vertex a shade sits on, names them all wrong with the winding reverted and **0** with it in. Two bodies of one species have the same silhouette, so the occlusion claim alone does not catch it: a wound-inward build moves the kept share from **0.022** of the far body left to **0.041** and stays inside its bounds.
- **Frustum culling**: 6 planes extracted from the MVP (row-major rows: `row3 ± row0/1/2`), tested against a **box** per chunk -- the chunk's own 16 x 48 x 16, and against each plane only the one corner of it that points furthest along that plane's normal, which is all a box ever has to answer. It used to be a sphere of radius **15** centred at half the map's height, and a chunk's own corners are **26.5** out from that centre, so a chunk whose only piece on screen was one of them was thrown away whole and the sky showed through the hole. A lens just above Mirrorlake, looking down at 69 degrees, asked the world what stood in front of it and found nothing drawn on **56,633** of the pixels that had an answer: a grey wedge of dry sky at the far corner of a chunk of lakebed, exactly where the lakebed is deepest. Two other lenses over the same water put **75,369** and **42,186** pixels of the same hole in the same frame. The test is a frame against itself -- the same lens drawn with the culling the game runs, and drawn with none at all -- and the two now agree byte for byte at eight lenses, which is worth saying because those eight do throw whole chunks away: between **40** and **88** of the valley's 100 a frame.
- **Emissive layer**: a separate mesh, raised by `emisBoost`, unaffected by day/night lighting.
- **Water**: a separate mesh, and it carries no alpha at all. The surface used to be a sheet of coloured glass laid over the frame: `BLOCKS[WATER].alpha` said **0.72**, folded once into the fourth byte of the vertex colour by `def()` and never written a second time at the draw call, and at 0.62 the lakebed kept 38% of its own contrast through it and the lake read as a pit in the ground rather than as water. It is gone. What water does to light is one law, per colour: `fogK: [1.40, 0.40, 0.15]` on the block row says how fast this stuff eats daylight per metre, colour by colour, and the same law answers every journey light makes through a lake. That row used to say `[0.55, 0.16, 0.06]` -- the absorption of a mountain tarn -- and from its bank this lake was an aquarium: a lens 40 cm over the surface picked the blocks of a bed 6 m down out of the water, the bed keeping **0.29** of the spread it has when it is dry, and the whole floor of Mirrorlake was legible from the shore. The green is the channel the eye reads brightest and the one that decides how far down one can see, and it now eats **0.40** per metre, which puts this lake's own visibility near **2.5 m**: from the bank you see water, with a suggestion of a floor under it. The ratio between the three colours is the one the row has always had -- that ratio is why the floor is teal and not grey -- only how fast the whole thing happens was wrong. (The numbers quoted further down this file were measured through this lake: the ones the current test log still produces are re-pinned where they stand, and the rest are the record of the day each of them was caught.) The sun's way down, baked into the bed's vertices as `aUnder`. The way back up, asked of whatever surface the lens is looking at, and measured along the ray the light travels rather than straight up. The water the lens itself sits in, which is the haze. And the water between the lens and the surface it is looking through, asked of that face. The lake used to answer that one question three times over -- the alpha, a haze copied from the air's squared law at 0.14 per metre, and the baked absorption -- and the three disagreed by **48** luminance at two metres of water. The alpha was also the only thing the lake had between a lens and the sky, so it was drawn only when the lens was out of the water and cost exactly nothing from under it: it hid a bright shallow bed while brightening a dark deep one. Vertex waves: `sin(t*1.7 + x*0.9 + z*0.7)*0.055 + sin(t*0.6 + z*0.25)*0.03`. The surface is drawn **un-culled** and **twice**. Un-culled because it is one quad per block face, so seen from underneath it is a back face and the culler -- on for the whole frame -- threw it away: standing on the bed of a three-block-deep column, the brightest pixel in the frame was **255** and **2.73%** of it was over 200/255, which was the sun seen straight through the lake; now **128** and **0%**. Twice because how much of the light survives is per colour while a blend can only scale the whole frame by one number: the first pass multiplies the frame by `survival x (1 - F)`, the second adds what the face throws back and what the water scatters into the ray on its way out. A lake is a closed volume and the mesher only emits a face where water meets air, so no pixel can ever see both sides of the same quad: no double blending, no extra geometry.
- **A lake is a mirror from the bank and a window from a boat**: the surface's reflectance is Fresnel, `0.02 + 0.98 * pow(1 - cn, 5)`, asked of the face the lens is looking at rather than of the lens, so the same water throws most of its view back when it is looked at along and lets nearly all of it through when it is looked at down. Two lenses 30 cm above one three-metre shelf, at noon, over the pixels the water covers: the grazing one mean luminance **94** against the steep one's **59.8**, and the grazing one is also the bluer (**125.7** against **93**) because what comes back off it is the sky. The dry bed under the same two lenses is the other way round -- **171.6** and **178.7**, blue **126.3** and **129.8** -- which is the whole of the difference between a surface and a floor.
- **Glass**: `BLOCKS[GLASS].alpha` has said 0.34 since the first commit and nothing ever read it. Glass rode in the opaque mesh, so the castle greenhouse, the windows of the sunken tower and every window in Lanternrow were painted blue-grey slabs you could not see through and could not walk into. A block that is neither opaque nor liquid now rides a sixth mesh of its own, drawn after the bodies so the depth they wrote decides whether a body shows through a window, and its alpha arrives as the fourth byte of the vertex colour. Faces between two neighbouring blocks of the same transparent kind are still culled, so a wall of glass is one pane thick rather than one pane per brick.
- **The lake has a colour from the shore**: the water surface used to be the only blended draw in the game, and the world shader divided the alpha byte by 255 *after* the attribute pointer had already normalized it, which left the surface 0.4% opaque. Standing outside Mirrorlake you saw no lake at all, and the only water with a colour was the fog you breathe with your head under. Every other test reads the world; this one has to read the frame: draw, draw again with nothing the water does left anywhere in the frame -- the law, the haze, the colour the water scatters back into the ray and the surface itself, which leaves the bed as dry sand -- and the two frames must differ, over a tenth of the picture (**49,621** pixels), and differ towards the water's own blue (**47,856** of those pixels bluer than red with the water and not without it). The claim is the colour, not the brightness: over the pixels the water covers, the bed's red falls to **0.17** of what it is dry, its green to **0.45** and its blue keeps **0.711**. A tint painted over the frame takes the same fraction off every channel; the lake does not, and that is the difference between water and shade. The older question -- how much of the bed's own spread survives the surface -- meant nothing when it was asked, because the lake was an aquarium then: the surface put as much variation into the picture as the bed lost, so over a three-metre shelf the wet frame was **2.7** times as varied as the dry bed under it. Asked again with the row eating at the rate it eats now, that question is the one that decides whether a lake hides its floor, and it is asked of the same bed with nothing the water does left anywhere in the frame: a bed 6 m down keeps **0.045** of the spread it has dry, a bed 4 m down keeps **0.297**.
- **Sky**: a full-screen quad, procedural gradient + sun/moon discs + stars. The quad rebuilds a view direction per pixel from the camera basis — `vDir = normalize(uFwd + uRight·aCorner.x·tanX + uUp·aCorner.y·tanY)` — so the basis the renderer hands the shader has to be the same handedness the view matrix was built from. It was not: `begin()` computed `up = fwd × right`, which is `right × fwd` (what `M4.lookYaw` uses) negated. Only the sky reads `up`, so every block, body and particle was where it should be while the sky alone was mirrored about the horizon: the top of the screen sampled *below* the horizon and got the ground colour, and the sun's glow sat under the horizon. At 07:00, lens level, facing the sun's azimuth with the sun 31° up, the brightest pixel in a 300 px frame sat 135 px below the centre. Stars are gated on the sun being **down** (`smoothstep` on `-uSunDir.y`), not on `uNight`: the day/night curve is symmetric about noon, so at 07:00 `uNight` still says 37% night while the sun is 29° up, and 217 star spikes were measured in a daylight sky next to the sun.
- **Fog**: exponential, colour follows day/night; underwater changes colour and density — and "underwater" is read off the **lens's** own cell, not the body's head; inside the Grey Veil it becomes dense grey fog.
- **The dark of a night is the sky's light**: at midnight every surface was lit by `uDay` alone — 0.16 of its own colour — and by nothing else, so a dark material (conifer `[0.14, 0.30, 0.20]`, dark leaf, roof tile) came out at luminance 7–10 while the night sky behind it was 17–21: a tree or a roof read as a hole cut in the sky. Measured on four night lenses: **4–42%** of the frame below luminance 12, darkest surface **6.8**, sky **17.3–21.3**; the same four lenses at 13:00 have **0%** below 12. The sky's own colour is now **added** to every surface (`uAmb`, from `env.ambient`), gated on how much of the sun's light is left on the ground. Added rather than multiplied, because that is the shape the problem has: it lifts a dark surface by most of its own value and a bright one by hardly anything. After: **0%** below 12 on all four lenses, darkest surface **15.3–17.5** against a sky of **18–21.3**; and a daytime frame is bit for bit what it was, because `ambient` is `[0,0,0]` from 08:00 through 16:00. **The gate was asked of the sun's height, and that is where the black mass at dusk came from.** The gate this used opened at an elevation of 0 and closed at −0.2 — on this clock 18:00 and 18:46 — while the sun's own light on the ground (`day`) is down to **22%** at 18:00 and gone at **18:32**, and the sky is at its brightest across those hours, because `dusk` peaks as the sun crosses the horizon. Through the whole of dusk the ground was therefore lit by a fraction of the sun's light and by none of the sky's, and the HUD calls 18:00 **night** (`night` 0.78 > 0.55): the game named it night while drawing the blackest ground it has. Measured at 18:00 on four lenses aimed at the horizon, the share of the frame that is geometry below luminance 24 against a sky of **61–75** was town **38.4%**, grove **30.4%**, ridge **38.4%**, lake **2.6%**, and the darkest 1% of that geometry was **0.21–0.31** of the sky's mean. Asked of `1 - day` instead — of the light left on the ground rather than of where the disc is — all four hold **0%** and that ratio is **0.62–0.80**. A ceiling of 0.60 was tried and refused: at midnight the darkest 1% rises to **0.97–1.11** of the sky and the night flattens into a grey. The two ends of the clock do not move: `day` is 1 from **08:00 to 16:00**, where the term was and stays `[0,0,0]`, and it is 0 from **18:32 to 05:28**, where the term is the **0.42** the heart of the night has always had; what changes is the hour on either side of dusk and dawn.
- **The bottom of a lake is under water**: the water put a haze between the lens and a surface, and that was all it did -- and a haze works over distance, so at the lake's density a fragment 3 m away took 18% of it and one 10 m away took 86%. The lakebed a body was swimming over therefore kept the dry sand colour it has on the shore. Measured looking straight down from just under the surface, the frame was **warmer than red** (blue-minus-red **-16.7**) and **brighter** (mean **137**) than the same lens looking *up* at the sunlit surface (mean **90**) -- the bottom of a lake brighter than its own sunlit skin. What was missing is that the light reaching a surface under water has itself come down through the water above it: the red goes first. `BLOCKS[WATER]` now carries `fogK: [1.40, 0.40, 0.15]` -- how fast this stuff eats daylight, per metre, colour by colour -- and the shader multiplies a surface's daylight by `exp(-fogK x depth under the surface)`. **The depth is the surface's own, not the lens's.** For the static world it is measured when the mesh is built: `buildChunk` walks every column of the chunk **plus a ring of one**, up front, and keeps each column's surface height and its lowest wet cell in two small typed arrays -- the same columns the lazy walk ended up visiting anyway, since every face vertex asks for its own column and the four it shares a face with, and a horizontal face asks for the column under the block it belongs to, which stands on that corner's diagonal -- and every vertex inside that span is uploaded with how far under it sits, in 1/8 m steps, as a third byte beside the shade and the emissive flag (`aUnder`). **Only 24 of the valley's 100 chunks hold water, and the 76 that do not are now handed no water question at all**: they were paying five map lookups for every one of their vertices to be told nothing. A chunk rebuild cost **4.15 ms** on average and **15.4 ms** in the worst chunk, and one fruit picked in that chunk cost **22.5 ms** of frame; after, **1.05 ms** on average. Which chunk is the worst one is not a number this machine can give: four runs of this same sweep named four different chunks and read **1.8 ms**, **1.9 ms**, **2.1 ms** and **4.8 ms** for them, so the test holds the worst chunk to a **12 ms** backstop rather than to a ceiling it cannot measure. `game/test/play.js` reads the depth byte back off the meshes the mesher uploads and checks every one of them against the lake, column by column (**0** wrong of **51,814** vertices that carry water), shows that **443** of them could only have come from a column in the next chunk over, and counts the map lookups that sweep pays over the whole valley: **0** for the **1,105,482** vertices it uploads, where the same sweep used to pay **15,982,908** of them. The valley rebuilds in **104 ms** to **122 ms** -- **0.094 ms** to **0.11 ms** per thousand vertices, against **508 ms** and **0.46 ms** -- and what moves across that span is the rest of the test suite running through the same machine, not the mesher. **That column question is the wrong question for one kind of face, and a lakebed is made of it.** A face the mesher can see standing against water hands its surface height in instead, because a corner of a submerged face can sit inside the solid next door, where no column of water reaches down to it: the point question then answers "dry", and the corner is lit the colour of a beach. 852 of the 7,528 faces this mesher puts against water had at least one corner answered that way -- 1,981 corners, the worst at (74, 4, 95) with the lake's surface 6 m above it -- and in the frame they were the strange bright dots on the lakebed: a lens just above the surface, looking down, sees 5,337 pixels of bed through at least two metres of water, sorted into three bands by how deep the face stands under it, and in the shallowest band **12** of 1,098 came back warmer than blue, **7** brighter than 140, the brightest **162** against a band whose own average is 59. Asked of the face instead, every corner is answered and every one of those 12 is gone: **0** warmer than blue, **0** over 140, brightest **60** in every band, and the three bands keep their order -- 55.4, 51.5, 51.0 as the floor goes down. A vertex still takes the deepest of its own column and its four neighbours, which is what makes the face of a rock the lake covers wet while the roof of a cave under the lakebed is not. **And the corner of a micro box is not on a column line at all.** A stalk of kelp stands **inside** a column, at a fractional x, z and height, and the mesher used to hand every such corner no water whatever: 10,656 micro corners over the 24 wet chunks, 5,328 of them under the surface, every one of them lit by full noon -- which cost nothing for as long as nothing micro stood in the lake, and costs the lake its whole bed the day the bed starts growing kelp. The corner now asks the column it stands in, and the depth is uploaded in 1/8 m steps taken by truncation, which is exact for a block corner and not for a stem standing at y+0.2, so the audit truncates the same way. The coefficient itself reaches the frame once, as `env.liqK` off `liquidAbsorb()`. For the things that are not baked -- a body, whose vertices are rebuilt every frame, and a fruit dropped on the lakebed -- the draw is handed the surface of the column *that thing* stands in (`liquidSurfaceAt()`, and for a body its own `wSurf`, which it collects in its own update); the outline box round the block you are looking at is handed none, because it lights itself, and a thing that lights itself is not lit by the sun coming down through the lake. The world's own meshes are handed none of it either: their depth came in with them. **The world owns the reading of a column**: `liquidSurfaceAt()` is its surface as a height and `waterTop()` in the physics file is the same column as a *cell*, and both answer **-1** for a column holding no water, so the two conventions cannot disagree about whether a column is in the lake at all -- swept over the lake, **2,949** wet columns and **1,407** dry ones, and none in disagreement. The exponent is linear in the depth, and deliberately not the haze's squared-exponential law: the haze is scattering over the viewing path (nothing in the first metre, everything at a hundred), while the sunlight coming down is being absorbed, and absorption starts eating the red from the first metre -- which is exactly where a body wading over a four metre shelf is looking. **After**, at noon, a lens 60 cm under the surface of a six-metre shelf: looking down, blue-minus-red **+61.6** at mean **45.9**; looking up at the sunlit skin, **+108.5** at mean **68.4** -- the skin brighter than the floor, which is the order it has to be in; and on a bed 9 m down, **+56.3** at mean **46.8**. **The floor at arm's length is the same question, and the haze cannot answer it**: the lens there is standing on the bank, out of the water, so there is no haze between it and that bed at all -- what reaches the bed is the one law asked of the surface the lens is looking through. Measured on the patch straight in front of the crosshair, not over the whole frame, which averages the far lake in with it: a bed two metres down reads blue over red by **+80** at mean luminance **57**. With the law switched off the same patch is red over blue by **69** at mean **187** -- the colour of a lakebed that has never been under water. The same patch with the bed five metres deeper reads **+58** at mean **46**: it keeps losing red as the floor goes down while its blue holds, which is the difference between a law and a tint. **And what is left down there is the veil, not the floor**: the same column drawn one depth at a time, the lens 40 cm over its surface, and the frame drawn again with nothing the water does left anywhere in it -- which is the only way to ask how much of a floor survives without asking how bumpy that particular floor is. The spread across the patch is what the eye reads as relief, because a floor is blocks and water is not, and it falls to **0.297** of the dry bed's at four metres down and to **0.045** of it at six; and the six-metre patch has come to within a luminance of the colour the water scatters back into the ray (**48.5** against the water's own **49.7**) while the four-metre one still stands **3** above it. **The way back up is measured along the ray, not straight up**: that leg used to be the bed's own depth under the surface -- a vertical -- while the haze a lens sits in has always been measured along the ray, and which of the two a frame gets is decided by `Math.floor(lens.y)`, which flips at the surface line. A lens 5 cm under a four-metre shelf, looking at its bed along 7 degrees of slope, wore the whole veil; 5 cm over the same spot it was handed only the vertical depth, and the murk left the frame as the lens crossed the line. Measured on the bed 2-5 m in front of the crosshair, 3-4 m down, at noon: mean luminance and blue over red **69.8 / 80.6** with the lens 5 cm under the line, **81.3 / 71.3** at the line, **87.4 / 72.6** 5 cm over it and **88.6 / 67.0** 30 cm over -- the bed drier the higher the lens climbed, which is the bug -- and the bed's own red back at **31.3** and **42.4** where under the line it is **19.3**. The vertical is now put along the ray (`vert * d / abs(vEye.y)`, capped at the ray's own length: a four-metre slab looked at along 17 degrees of slope is 13 m of water, and the ray never walks 13 m of it). After, at the coefficient the row carries today, on the same bed 2-5 m in front of the crosshair: **53.9 / 71.7** with the lens 5 cm under the line, **54.1 / 71.8** at it, **54.2 / 72.0** 5 cm over and **62.0 / 74.0** 30 cm over -- mean luminance and blue over red, with the bed's own red at **15.3 / 15.4 / 15.4** for the first three and **22.8** for the last. Crossing the line costs the bed nothing, and the veil does thicken as the sight line starts to slope. Looked at nearly straight down, where the two measures are all but one and the same line, nothing moves across the boundary either: **61.1 / 61.1 / 61.4**. And the lamps: the shader discounts the haze for a fragment that lights itself, so that a lantern across the valley is still a lantern -- and that discount was a constant **0.75** written in the shader, in which the lake had no say. The lanternfish swim the open water of the lake (they used to sit on the outline of the sunken tower), so a body that swims in among them faces them wherever the school happens to be: 3.6 m off the school the frame came back with pixels at luminance **246**, its 99th percentile at **180** and **0.72%** of it over 200/255 -- a sun sitting on the lakebed. The discount is now `fogEmis` on the water row (**0.15**), reaching the shader as `env.emisKeep`, with the air's 0.75 as the default so a frame drawn in air is bit for bit what it was. After, at the coefficient the row carries today: that school of lamps at mean **49.6**, its 99th percentile **101**, and nothing at all over 200/255.
- **Particles**: a single draw call, capped at 900 (fireflies by night, bubbles under water, and the sparks a transformation lets go). A mote is not a surface: it is *added* to the frame, so the water cannot colour it over -- what the water does to it is eat part of the light the mote sends out on its way to the lens, and until now a mote was asked nothing at all. Sixty bubbles let go 0.4-2.9 m under Mirrorlake, 4-8 m from a lens sitting 60 cm under the surface of the same lake, came back over **429** sampled pixels at mean luminance **140**, **197** of those pixels over 140 and the brightest **222**, while the lakebed behind them, at the same lens, reads **66..70** -- the same strange bright dot the lakebed used to be, still floating there after the lakebed itself had been fixed. After: **303** pixels, mean **101**, their red **69** down to **20**, brightest **150**, and **15** over 140. (Those numbers were taken with the coefficient the water row carried that day. The row eats faster now, and a mote under the lake is dimmer than that record says -- what has not moved is the ratio the law keeps between a mote seen through the lake and the same mote with the law switched off, which is what the step measures today: a mote 5.5 m down keeps **4** of its red where the same mote with the law off keeps **127**, and its brightest contribution falls from **171** to **67**.) How far a mote sits under the surface of its own water arrives as an attribute the way the static world's depth does, measured at the moment of drawing rather than at spawn, because a bubble rises and the lake it has already climbed out of is gone. And the walk is measured **along the ray**: a bubble hung 2.5 m under the surface keeps **32** of its red looked at from straight above, and **2** looked at along 20 degrees of slope from the same distance -- 7 m of lake rather than 2.5. The water the lens itself stands in asks the same question: a mote 8 m off inside the lake keeps **2** of its red where one 2 m off keeps **42**, and a splash a metre over the skin keeps **20** seen from 3 m down against **70** seen from 30 cm down. A mote in the air seen from the air walks no water at all: exp(0) is 1, and the frame it is drawn into is byte for byte the frame that was drawn before this law existed. A bubble also pops where it reaches the surface instead of going on through the open air: it is the one buoyant thing the valley spawns (`grav < 0` -- motes and dust hover at 0, embers fall at 1.2), and it used to rise clean out of the lake and float on for the rest of its life. Ten seconds of a body whose head sits 30 cm under the surface of a four metre shelf: **2,770** of **3,834** bubble-samples were **above** the surface of the lake, the highest **5.84** m over the water; the same ten seconds with the body standing on the bed four metres down, **1,455** of **3,261**, up to **3.68** m over it. After: none, and the cell test that decides it costs one read per mote per tick. The span the spawner scatters bubbles over is the water the body stands in now -- feet first, no higher than 25 cm under the skin -- so a bubble is not born in the open air only to be killed a tick later (**28** of **47** births were in water before, **30** of **43** after). The night's motes are put down by the game rather than found in the world, and the column read that puts them down answers "the highest solid thing in this column" -- over a lake that is the lakebed: ninety seconds standing on the shore of Mirrorlake let go **28** of **76** fireflies under the surface of the lake and **16** inside a block, and **34%** of the motes the night kept alive glowed through up to **8.4 m** of water. The surface of the water is the floor to lift off from where there is one (`liquidSurfaceAt`, and the higher of the two answers wins), and a column with no room left above its highest solid leaves the mote unmade rather than buried. And a mote keeps to the air it was put in. A mote is drawn, not walked: it has no size and no mass, and the frame it is added into has already been depth-tested, so a mote inside a block is not seen *through* the block -- it is simply not there, holding a slot off the 900 and fading where nobody can see it. The valley was spending a great deal of its light that way: standing on a lake shore at night, **703** of **12,917** mote-samples were inside a block; a spark burst thrown at open ground buried **286** of **4,109** samples under that ground, and **426** of **4,080** thrown up under a crown. The world is a wall to a mote the way it is a wall to a body: the move into a solid is refused and the mote spends itself against the face it reached -- one read, the same one the bubble rule above already pays -- and the wall is asked of the cell the mote is **stored** in rather than of the double that carried it there: the step is computed in double and the position is float32, and a destination a hair below a cell boundary in double stores as the boundary itself, which is how one mote a while, born in the air under a crown and rising, crossed the face of a solid cell while its double destination was still the air cell under it; the stored position was the solid, and a mote already inside a block keeps its move by the rule that follows, so it walked through the cell it was let into and spent its whole remaining life inside it -- while a mote the world has already closed around keeps its move, so that it can still work its way out (**19** frames out of a needle). And the ring the night's motes are scattered over reaches eighteen metres out from the body, which near the edge of the map is off the map, where `get` answers "air" to a question that was never asked: standing eight blocks in from the edge, **85** of **682** fireflies were let go outside the valley and **21,863** of **149,080** samples (**14.7%**) were spent there, glowing at nothing. After: none of these, at any of the spots.
- **Camera**: the lens points exactly where the mouse points, in both views. What trails behind is the body (`faceYaw`), which has mass. A lens that eased toward the aim was the bug: W ran off in a direction the screen was not pointing at, and the highlight box sat over a block the crosshair was not on. `cam.yaw` / `cam.pitch` are gone — the only camera state left is the distance and the field of view, both eased.
- **Third-person distance**: `1.5 + 2.25 × height`, eased, plus 8° of field of view — the subject keeps roughly the same share of the frame whether it is a frog or a person.
- **Occlusion**: a DDA from the subject toward the lens, solid blocks only (fruit and reeds must not blind the camera). The lens is backed off to the **near face** of the hit block, standing 0.55 m short of it when that keeps the lens at least 0.08 m off the subject; a face nearer than 0.63 m leaves the lens at that 0.08 m, in front of the face. It used to stand 0.2 m short of the face with a floor 0.55 m off the subject, and a face nearer than 0.75 m drove the lens to the floor -- 0.25 m **inside** the block, where back-face culling throws the near face away and the wall simply vanishes from the frame: orbiting the body beside a wall, a trunk or the tower was the way to meet it. Now the lens is never inside a block, and the face it stands in front of never crosses the 0.06 m near clip the engine draws behind, below which it stops being drawn and the wall you are pressed to vanishes instead of filling the frame.
- **Inside your own body**: pulled in that far, the lens is inside the box it is filming, so the body is not drawn — otherwise you are looking at the inside of your own skull.
- **The view follows the lens, the body keeps breathing**: a third-person lens trails the body, and it trails it *down*. Standing on a shore shelf with the lake surface at y = 15 and a dry head at 16, the lens sat at **14.19** — under the water. The view was keyed to the body, so that lens was handed a dry frame: **3.75%** of it over 200/255, brightest pixel **255**, while **0 of 56,100** rays cast from it reached the sky — every one hit water first. The frame is now tinted by the water it is really looking through: mean luminance **142 -> 93.7**, blue-minus-red **+33.9 -> +111.5**, brightest pixel **255 -> 168**, over 200/255 **3.45% -> 0%** -- the water's own law switched on for the frame (`hazeOn 1`, `hazeK [1.40, 0.40, 0.15]`) and the water's own veil colour `[0.06, 0.24, 0.32]` taken outright instead of mixed 85% into the sky's. The body still owns breath, wetness and bubbles: in that same frame the head above the surface stays dry and the air still fills (air left above **5**). A level view, lens above the surface, is untouched -- the air's own `fogDen 0.0075` before and after, and the frame keeps its sky: **4,132** of those same 56,100 rays reach it. The haze belongs to the block now: `fogC / fogT / fogEmis / fogK` on the water row of the block table, read through `liquidFogAt()` -- the environment code used to carry a second, unlinked copy of the water's colour. A liquid that states a per-colour law (`fogK`) replaces the air's squared-exponential haze outright, and the air's own density is left alone; a liquid that states only a density keeps the air's shape and takes the density instead. So a second liquid -- a swamp, a lava -- would say all four of those things about itself and the environment code would not be edited at all; the one thing it would have to say that is baked in is its own absorption, which is measured into the world's vertices when they are built, and so would have to be baked alongside the depth rather than read off the frame.
- **The body is wet in the half that is under the water**: the body being filmed used to be the last thing drawn, after the water pass, so it rode on top of the lake's own blend and stayed dry from its feet to its head. Standing in a one-block shore shelf with the lens 5.55 m out over open water and 2.1 m above the surface, switching the water meshes off moved the lakebed behind the body by **148** on average, while the **642** pixels of the body below the surface line moved by **17** and only **13.2%** of them moved at all. The body now rides with the other bodies, before the glass and the water: **100%** of those pixels move, by **32.3** on average, and the half of the body under the surface reads blue over red by **+12.9** where the dry half of the same body in the same frame reads **-39**. The half above the surface line is untouched — **0** pixels changed, before and after — because the water writes no depth and cannot reach a fragment the body already drew in front of it. It tints the body without hiding it: **99.4%** of the body's silhouette still reads against the frame behind it.
- **One camera per frame**: `update()` shoots it once (`_camFrame`) and the picking ray, the highlight box, the interaction prompt and `render()` all read that same frame. The ray starts at the lens in third person and at the eye in first, but reach (5.5 m) is always measured from the body, never from the lens.
- **The opening frame belongs to the map**: `generate()` decides where the game opens **and which way the lens is turned**, and hands it over as `N.spawn` with a `yaw` on it; `init()` uses that yaw instead of a constant. The old rule only tested the column the player lands in and the two beside it — it never tested the direction the camera is pointing at open. It accepted a spot half a block from the main cellar's dark brick box, and 91% of the first frame was a wall 0.6 m away at a mean luminance of 57/255 with no sky in it at all. Walking away is what made it look better a few seconds later. The rule now casts a fan of rays (8×10, 76° field, 1.6 aspect) in each of the four cardinal directions from every street column near the cellar door, and takes the nearest column where at least one direction has **≥8% sky and ≤15% of pixels closer than 2 m**; if the village ever gets dense enough that no column passes, it takes the least bad one (≥3% sky, ≤30%) rather than dropping the player through a wall. Lanternrow now opens on the lantern road, 6.0 blocks from the cellar door, looking west along it. One lantern post is skipped along the way: the spacing put a post at x = 136, which is the cellar door's own column, so the door opened onto a lamp. `node-smoke.js` checks the spawn column is not buried and that the sight line is clear for 3 blocks — but that check passed on the old spot too, because its sight line went straight through the open doorway; only the frame check in `play.js` (the **whole** frame, not its upper half) sees a face pressed against a wall.

Measured in the prototype (1280×720, SwiftShader software rendering): all 100 chunks built, about 200–300k triangles, 47–400 draw calls, **54–60 fps**.

### 3.3 Data and saving

- Voxels: `Uint8Array(w*h*d)` = 160×48×160 = 1.23 MB.
- **Heightmap**: planned (`Uint8Array(w*d)`), not in the prototype. Bounding the meshing loop with one measured no faster, and it has to be right on every block write or geometry gets quietly clipped. Meshing scans the full chunk height; the time goes into the faces, not into the empty sky -- and the water table above is what proved it: the empty sky was being walked a second time, five map lookups for every vertex, and taking that out left **0.97 ms** for a chunk rebuild (**4.15 ms** before), about **0.09 ms per thousand vertices**.
- Save: `localStorage['frutaria.save.v3']`, storing only the **block diff** (`diffs`) + player (position, vitality, current form and its remaining time) + **identity** (`id` / `name`, see 03 §6) + backpack + storages (every field stash records the `owner` id that planted it) + regrow queue (`regrow`) + planting queue (`growQueue`) + fruit scattered by death (`drops`) + backpack upgrades + deeds + time. Measured 2.3 KB with a cellar tree and a sapling. It is written by the Save button and by `Ctrl+S`, and the page writes it **on its way out** (`pagehide`) and **when it is put to sleep** (`visibilitychange`): between one save and the next the valley exists only in that tab, so a reload, a closed tab or a phone switched to another app must not cost the player everything since the last save. Both hooks are gated on the game having started — a page still sitting on the start screen is holding a valley it has not loaded, and writing that fresh world would overwrite the save the screen is offering back. `F5` stays bound for the browsers that hand that key to the page; the ones that keep it for themselves lose nothing either, because the page has already written the valley as it goes.
- A save is **offered back, never silently loaded**: the start screen quotes it (day, hour, pack, trees) and Continue walks you back to it. The town cellar belongs to the valley rather than to the save, so it is rebuilt from the map data and every store is looked up by `id` — a save that lists only cellar trees still has a town to walk into. A save is also **read as data rather than as memory**: `localStorage` hands back whatever is in it -- the shape some other build wrote, a value a browser put down wrong, something typed into the console -- so every field the game indexes into is asked, before it is used, whether it is an object, a number, a list, or a row of four numbers pointing at a cell inside the valley. What cannot be answered stays at its default: a body wearing a fruit the valley no longer has wakes up as itself, a queue holding a null is dropped rather than thrown into `update()` every frame, and a save that is not an object is not offered back at all. Measured: eight shapes the game never wrote, each read by the same `load()` the Continue button calls and then run for a second of world time -- the body is somewhere, the clock is moving, nothing throws.

### 3.4 Interface language

The interface is switchable between Chinese and English at run time (`L`, or the button in the HUD); the choice is stored in `localStorage['frutaria.locale.v1']` and, on a first visit, taken from the browser language.

Two kinds of text exist and they are translated differently, on purpose:

| Kind | Where it lives | Looked up with | Examples |
|---|---|---|---|
| **interface text** | `data/i18n.js`, one flat key → string table per language | `N.t('hud.cap', {u, c})` | buttons, panels, prompts, log lines |
| **setting text** | on the record itself, in `data/fruits.js` / `creatures.js` / `world.js` / `player.js`, as a `cn` / `en` pair | `N.disp(record)` | a fruit's name and lore, a creature's dex entry, a zone name, an epithet |

Setting text stays next to the thing it describes because those data files are the source of truth the documents are checked against (`docs-sync.js` compares the prose in `docs/` with them). If the translations were moved into the language tables, the documents could drift away from the setting unnoticed.

Two consequences worth knowing:

- **Nothing translated is ever stored.** A store has no name of its own — the interface calls it by kind (`store.cellar` / `store.tree`) — and a deed is a flag next to the check that seals it, with no text at all. So a save written in Chinese loads unchanged in English, and switching language cannot freeze a string into a warehouse. `game/test/lang.js` asserts the save file is byte-identical across languages.
- **Adding a language is one table.** `data/i18n.js` holds `LANGS` plus one object per language; `node-smoke.js` fails the build if the tables do not cover exactly the same keys.

### 3.5 Known technical debt

| Item | Note |
|---|---|
| No occlusion culling | blocks outside are still drawn while you are inside a cave |
| No async meshing | chunk meshing is budgeted at 22 ms per frame during boot; a player edit flags its chunk and the flagged chunks are re-meshed on a 6 ms per frame budget -- and a chunk rebuild costs about **1.2 ms** here, so one harvest never spends the whole budget (**22.5 ms** for the two chunks a pick flagged before the water table was built up front) |
| Creatures have no pathfinding | no graph, no A*: they steer toward their errand at their own turn rate, and when the step in front is solid they take the way round that closes on the errand most -- the contour of the obstacle, not a fresh errand -- resting only when no way round does |
| No LOD / no streaming | the 160×48×160 world is fully resident in memory; the full scale needs chunk streaming |
| No NPC dialogue trees | Old Mei / One-Eye only have log text, no branching dialogue |
| The frog does not dry out on screen | `dryTimer` is in the data and the check is wired up, but there is no skin-moisture bar in the HUD (04 §3) |
| No identity service | the `id` is generated locally, not issued by a server; cellar trees already carry an `owner`, but the main cellar is communal and there is no sharing-rights model (see 03 §6) |
| Only two languages | a third language means one more flat table in `data/i18n.js` plus a third field on every record that carries setting text (`cn` / `en` pairs in the data files) |
| Zone prose is never displayed | every zone in `data/world.js` carries a `desc`, and no interface reads it. The map bible in 02 is the only place that writing reaches a reader, and it is Chinese-only, so it is not covered by the language switch either |
