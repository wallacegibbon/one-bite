# 03 · Characters and Creatures

Source of truth: `game/data/creatures.js` (2 species + the attitude matrix + codex text) and `game/data/player.js` (the identity layer: id / epithet / name, and `HUMAN_FORM` — the body you start in, which is a body record like the animals' and not a rendering detail).

**One species per ecological niche.** The valley has one opening you can wear: the shallows and the shore, and the frog takes it. There is no "second fox", and no in-between species that is "half otter, half merrow". A second niche — open water — is filled by an animal **you cannot wear**: the lamps of Mirrorlake bear no fruit, so they change nothing about what a door or a crevice means.

## 1. Characters

### The forager (16) — the name is the player's

A forager's apprentice from outside Lanternrow. **This character has no fixed name**: the valley does not address people by name, it addresses them by *what they have done* (see §6). Wherever these documents refer to the character they say "the forager" or use the second person.

- **No talent and no past**: an ordinary forager, subject to every law of the valley. Eating a second fruit while already transformed simply changes them into that body, like anyone else — one body at a time, for them too (04 §5.1).
- **No backstory**: this character did not come into the valley for a reason the game is going to explain. They are here, they need to eat, and the valley is full of things they cannot reach yet. That is the whole premise and there is nothing waiting behind it.

### Old Mei (老梅, ~70)

The keeper of the main cellar. She is the one who set the shape of Lanternrow's cellar: 96 slots, underground, constant temperature, spoilage rate ×0.1.

- Function: teaching, the backpack upgrade (Pouch, +6 slots), the only source of cellar stones.
- She is the only person in the valley who never transforms. The game never asks her why, and she never brings it up.

## 2. The creature attitude system

Attitude is not a numeric friendship score, it is the result of looking up **what shape you are in right now**.

| Attitude | Behaviour |
|---|---|
| `calm` | Ignores you, stays in your group |
| `ignore` | Does not react on its own, flees only when approached |
| `curious` | Comes closer to look (may lead you somewhere) |
| `flee` | Runs inside the `alert` radius |
| `hostile` | Charges / attacks inside the `alert` radius |

Lookup order: `attitude[species of the player's current form]` → `attitude.human` (when in human shape) → `attitude.default`. A creature meeting the same species as itself uses `self`.

### The full attitude matrix

Rows = creatures, columns = player forms. `·` = falls through to `default`.

| | human | frog |
|---|---|---|
| Mirrorlake Frog | · | calm |
| Lanternfish | · | · |

**There is no `lanternfish` column.** A column is a body the player is wearing, and the lamps bear no fruit, so nobody can arrive in one. The row exists because the animal exists; every cell falls through to `default`, which is `ignore`.

**Two design intentions you can read off the matrix:**

1. **The diagonal is all `calm`** — becoming that species is the pass. It is not a disguise: the grove and the lake read the body.
2. **No row contains `hostile`** — there are no monsters in the valley. Nothing here attacks the player on its own initiative any more: what is left of danger is the water, the height, and what your own body cannot do. All of it comes from the ground and from the body — none of it comes from what an animal intends. The lamps of Mirrorlake sting, and that is the same kind of danger rather than an exception to it: the animal does not react to you at all, it simply is stinging, the way the water simply is deep — and the answer is a body, not a weapon.

Distribution of the 18 individuals in the valley: frog 8, lanternfish 10.

**`turn` is agility, and it is a rate, not a stat**: the most radians a second this body may add to its heading **at its own cruising speed** — going half as fast it bends its course half as fast, because a turn is an arc and the speed sets how wide that arc can be. Nothing in the valley turns on the spot: the course bends, the body keeps going forward, and it gets where it was sent by carving rather than by being pointed. The form record of the same species carries the same number, because the animal and the body worn from it are one body.

Three things follow from that one field, and `play.js` measures each of them:

- **A turn costs speed, and the price is geometry.** To reach a place that lies close across, the animal has to slow until its own arc is tight enough to land there: the way to the target is a *chord* of the circle the body can hold, so the speed is `turn × d / (2 sin θ)`. Without that rule an animal sent to a place too close for its arc orbits it for the rest of the afternoon — measured: a frog sent to a place 1.2 m to one side walked 48 m round it and never came inside 0.79 m; with the rule the same 1.2 m takes 1 m of path.
- **One turn budget a frame, whoever spent it.** A body pressed against a cliff was being turned twice in the same frame — once by the steering, once by the slide along the wall — and came about at 3.2 rad/s on a record that says 1.6. The budget lands last, after everything else has had its say.
- **A walker goes round a wall, not into one.** When the next step is solid, the animal takes the passable side that points furthest back along the way it came and follows the contour; only when none of them does does it give up the target and rest. A body used to spend 80% of an afternoon standing against a cliff and now walks 94% of it. Bodies that live in water already slid along walls inside their own mode — this is the walking mode catching up.

**A body keeps the space it is in.** The water branch of the creature file, which eases a swimming body onto the level its column states, had never been asked: a fish put down on the lakebed at the bottom of the deepest column in the lamps' own disc -- **11 m** of water, film at 15, bed at 4 -- and let go closed the **9.31 m** to its level at a rate proportional to how much of it was left, **0.248 m** in a single frame, which is **14.9 m/s**, seven times the **2.2 m/s** its own row swims, with **64** of its 600 frames faster than that; held to that speed the same fish covers the same **9.31 m** inside the same ten seconds, its worst frame **0.0367 m** (**2.2 m/s**, its own speed exactly), and it arrives.
**A body is not a point, the ceiling answers last, and leaves are not a ceiling.** A gate that asks only about the cell under a body's feet lets it into a room it does not fit in, to stand there with its head in the beam. So the step a body takes asks whether the place it is about to put itself in is empty from its feet to its head, and the top of the map is such a ceiling: the sky's own roof sits one block under the top of the grid, so no body stands with its head through the ceiling of the valley. And a crown is not one of those things: leaves are neither a wall nor a roof to a body, any more than they are to a body that walks through a bush with its shoulders in it. The material is excused by what the block itself says rather than by a second list kept inside the collision code: `N.FOLIAGE` in `src/world.js` is every block the block table calls both `soft` and `solid` (7/8/9/27/28 — every leaf, needle, frond and willow the valley grows), and a trunk (6) is not in it because it is not soft, so it stays a wall and a roof to the body. The same table now answers for the animals too, and it was closed at the walk gate rather than in the table: a walker's step asks which surface its feet would stand on in that column (`standY`, which for a tree column answers the ground under the tree) and then the room over that surface, feet cell up to head cell, read out of that same material table. One cell over the surface was the whole of the difference between a wall and air, which is why the gate looked innocent: of the **2,684** wood cells of this valley **1,830** sit on wood and **843** stand on the ground, so in every one of them the cell over the surface a body stands on is wood and the step was refused; the remaining **11** are wood with open air straight under them, over that surface, and there the gate read air and the body walked in with its shoulders through the block. The one hopper the species table leaves — a frog, 0.43 m tall, so a block one cell over the floor it stands on is inside its own body and a block two cells up is over its head — is driven at a trunk block in its own span, one cell over the floor: its centre comes within **0.55 m** of the centre of the nearest wood cell, and it reads **0** frames with any part of it inside the wood, **0 m** through at the deepest, while it still walks its own path and goes round the block rather than freezing at it (**104** of its **480** frames more than 1.2 m from the column's centre) — the span is asked of the body, not of the block. The same block one cell over its head is walked under (**80** frames in that column, **0** inside), and two cells over it is open ground (**80** frames there). The body that is not on its floor — a frog in the middle of an arc — is answered by the roof instead. The flock of this valley, walked a whole day, reads **0** frames inside the hard world — rock, masonry, glass — while the crowns its feet walk are only counted and reported: the wood a body walks through is not the hard world, which is why the rig had to be built at all, and why a rig of one body has to pin the page it runs in: with the crowd taken away for the length of a rig and the middle of the herd the page leaves behind left loose, the three rigs of one frog — the block at three different heights, under three different draws — agreed with each other to the centimetre on a **6.05 m** route that never came nearer than **1.49 m** to the block. Wood is wood to a walker and to the body worn from that walker at last: leaves are excused by `N.FOLIAGE`, and a trunk (6) and bone (22) are not in it.

**A hop is an arc with a top, and the roof answers it.** A hop is the same question asked one frame before the legs push off: the arc the data asks for reaches 0.78 m over the feet and a frogpear stands 0.43 m tall, so the eight frogs of Mirrorlake — who walk in and out of the hollow shaft of the sunken tower, where the brick floor is at 11 and the tower's glass is a metre over it — do not push off in there. One of them did, and was lifted out of the wall at 0.1 m a frame for 28 frames, which is what a day of the valley was catching one run in six. A frog under a low roof walks, and its legs do not load for a jump it is not going to make. And the roof a body on foot asks is asked out of the same material table, which is what makes a beam a beam: a trunk over the floor a frog stands on is a roof, while leaves at the same height are not. Measured on one frog, one column, one block set two cells over its floor, 480 frames: under a trunk it put its head **0.16 m** into that block in **14** of those frames, coming within **0.11 m** of it; under a crown of leaves at the same height it touched nothing. Counted where the block is — the frames off the floor of the column it stands over, and the arcs pushed off in that column — a crown costs this hopper nothing: it launches **1** arc there and spends **29** airborne frames there. A trunk costs it both: **0** arcs and **0** airborne frames there, walking instead, because the arc that does not fit is never loaded. Counted over the whole eight seconds those two read **9** arcs and **249** against **264** airborne frames, because the hopper walks out of the column and hops about down the shelf: a control taken over the whole eight seconds reads the height and not the material. And with this table not asked, that trunk reads the crown's **1** arc and **29** frames exactly — which is the height being read and not the material. The same roof is now asked over the whole of the arc and not over the column the body stands in, because an arc goes forward as far as it goes up: that same frog walked at the block from next door launched itself in the open column, spent **26** airborne frames inside the column with the block over it without ever pushing off in it, and arrived with its head **0.16 m** inside that block on the frame after the push — **14** frames of it, nearest wood **0.31 m**. Asked over the cells the arc is spent over, the same rig walks **80** frames in that column, spends **145** airborne frames where it had **250**, launches **5** arcs where it had **9**, and touches nothing. Three halves are the law, and each is needed by a rig of its own: with the span alone missing the body stands **30** frames inside the block and no other rig moves; with the material table missing from the ceiling the frog under the block stands **14** frames inside it and the one walked at it is inside **28**; with the arc's forward reach missing the walked-at frog is inside **14** frames and the one under it is clean; with all three missing, **72** frames have part of a body inside wood, every one of them a head.

**A body keeps its own trunk's footprint from its own kind as well.** Every gate so far asks one body about the world, and nothing ever asked one body about another body: two frogs walked through one another, and one lamp was drawn on top of another lamp. The ground a body claims is not a radius -- it is the rectangle its own trunk is carved from (`N.MODEL_W` in `src/models.js`: the union of the boxes of `parts[0]`, in the model's own units) turned by its heading, and it counts only where the two bodies also stand at one height, because two lamps a metre apart down the water are not in one place, and a lamp riding on another's back is on top of it, not inside it. A person is 0.41 m side by side and 0.29 m nose to nose, a frog 0.34 across and 0.41 long, so the distance at which two bodies count as in one place depends on the way they stand to one another, and the rule asks it of the body, not of a circle. A neighbour is then an obstacle of the kind the contour already takes a body round: the step gate refuses the places that lie deeper in a neighbour than the place the body stands in now, so a body already against one may slide along it and a body not yet in its way may touch it, 2 cm of it; the errand is picked from the roomiest of the places offered, and room is measured against where each neighbour stands *and* where it is going, because a place another body is swimming to is claimed whether that body is in it yet or not; and whatever overlap a frame still ends with is undone along the shortest way out that the world allows, the world keeping the last word -- a lamp pushed out of a neighbour and into a bank stays on the bank's side of the bank. Measured over the morning the game opens on, in a valley born the way the game bears it: the ten lamps stood in one another's way in 17197 pair-frames of a window of 9000, 12995 of them more than 12 cm deep, the worst pair 0.54 m in -- one fish drawn almost exactly on top of another -- and the frogs spent 46% of the morning with another frog inside them, 0.43 m at the worst -- twelve of them when the morning was measured; the valley drives eight now. Now the worst pair is 0.202 m, the frames more than 12 cm deep are an eighth of what they were, and a pair that touches is 0.073 m inside itself instead of 0.242 m; the frogs are in one another's way in fewer frames than before as well, 1982 against 4099, because they go round one another now instead of through one another. Three laws had to be asked of that undo, and each number below comes from a run of the same seeded window with that one law taken out and the other two in force. The undo is bounded for a pair -- half the way out of that one overlap, at the rate the world's own corrections work at -- but it is taken once for every pair that overlaps, so a body standing inside several of its own kind is moved several times in one frame and nothing bounded the frame: over 150-second watches of the field a player actually meets, the worst frame moved a lamp 0.133-0.156 m at a sixtieth of a second, 7.9-9.4 m/s for an animal whose row says it swims at 2.2 and wanders at 0.92, and a rig that stepped that same field at a fixed fifth of a second measured five whole minutes averaging 17.9 m/s with a worst frame of 4.1 m (82 m/s) -- fish thrown about their own field for a whole watch. Each body now has one budget a frame, and the budget is its own speed: neighbours may move a body, never faster than the body can move itself. The worst frame of the measured window fell from 0.127 m -- 7.6 m/s, 3.46 times what that lamp's own fins would have carried it -- to 0.052 m, 3.1 m/s, 1.42 times. What it was charged when it went in was a fingernail at the worst pair, 0.229 m against 0.223, and what it bought was a fifth off the rest: 2856 frames deeper than 12 cm against 3653, and a mean depth of 0.084 against 0.091. Asked again in the valley as the water laws below leave it, the same law out of the same window gives a worst frame of 0.114 m -- 6.8 m/s, 3.1 times what that lamp's own fins would carry it -- against 0.052 m and 1.42 times with it in: the worst pair is the better for the budget (0.202 m against 0.243) and the mean depth of a pair that touches is the same either way (0.073), while the deep frames are not -- 820 with the law out against 1652 with it in. A slower undo leaves a deep overlap lying open longer; what it no longer does is throw a fish across its own field at three times the speed its own fins can go, and the check that pins the frame is the one the field is held to, because a fish flung across its field is not a lamp drifting. And the crowd that does not fit its ground used to be put down on top of itself: the lamp field is ten lamps sampled into a disc of radius four round the tower, 29 of its 49 columns are water and the rest is the tower's own brick, the room a lamp asks of its own kind is 0.83 m and ten lamps do not fit -- the birth took the first sample clear and otherwise whatever the tenth throw landed on, and in the worst of five mornings the nearest pair stood 0.23 m apart, fish inside fish. Of the places a species will accept, the one taken is now the one furthest from its own kind, the rule the animal already follows when it picks somewhere to swim to, and the worst nearest pair of the same five mornings is 0.67 m. What the ring cannot hold it still cannot hold, and that is reported rather than hidden: six of the ten were born with a neighbour inside that room and eight are now, while the nearest pair moved from 0.23 m to 0.67 m -- what the law refuses is not a lamp born close to a lamp, it is a fish born on top of a fish. The third law is asked of the ground a shove lands on rather than of the body. The room the keep-apart pass asked its candidates with was the ground a body on foot would stand on -- within a step of where it is, and clear above it -- which is the right question for a body on foot and the wrong one for a frog the water is holding a metre off a lakebed beside a bank whose stone runs up through the height it floats at. Four frogs of Mirrorlake packed closer than their own trunks, the front one 0.15 m off such a shore, were shoved into that stone: 8 frames of a quarter-of-a-minute window in which a body stood inside the bank, the front one inside block 4 at 47.96, and then the law further up lifting it out of the stone at 0.13 m a frame and on to the crown of the bank at 13.0, where it stayed -- a body the lake is holding up, stood on the bank's stone by its own neighbours -- and that is the handful of frames the flock step of the test suite has reported now and then, a frog inside a block for 1 to 5 of the 2400 wet frames of its window, on some pages and not on others. Now none of them, with the file still spread as far as the bank lets it (0.75 m front to back when the window opens and 1.07 m when it closes, the same both times the suite is run, now that the four are stood facing away from the face at the same point of their stroke instead of in whatever state an earlier step left them in) and the way round taken instead; and the price it was charged when it went in was the depth of the window above, because a body refused the way out it was offered must find another or stay where it is pressed: with that law out that window ended at 0.192 m at the worst pair and 2089 frames deeper than 12 cm, with it in at 0.229 m and 2856. Asked again in the valley as the water laws below leave it, that guard costs nothing at all -- the same window reads 0.202 m and 1652 deep frames with it, and 0.202 m and 1652 without it -- and it still earns its keep where it was written: the packed file spends 8 frames of its window inside the stone without it and 0 with it.

**An animal is put down on the ground, not on the tree that grows out of it.** A crown and the trunk that holds it up are solid — the player climbs them and a body can stand on them — but they are not the surface of the valley. The spawn code scans a column from the top of the world, and it used to answer "the top of a spruce" in 509 of 600 tries: two bodies standing on crowns at y=45. `terrainY` reads the column for the ground, and `N.TREE_BLOCK` (`src/world.js` — the list `worldgen` has always used to plant a tree) says which blocks a tree is made of, so the generator and the animals read one list. It is not the answer to "may a body go through this": that is `N.FOLIAGE`, in the same file, and it is the leaves and not the trunk (s3). Two cases are counted rather than failed, and both are honest: a body's shoulders sit inside a bush as it walks through one, and where a crown grows to the top of the world there is no air left above it for a body to keep.

**A body on foot stands on the ground, and falls when the ground is gone.** `standY` is the third question asked of a column, after "the highest solid thing in it" and "the ground under the crown": *what surface are these feet standing on*. The two older readings disagree by metres and the animals were living in the gap — the altitude looked up 4 m above the feet while the step test looked up 3, so a needles block five blocks over a body's head answered to the altitude and lifted it there; and foliage level with the feet read as a step, so the body took it, found the next block of the same crown at its new feet, took that one too, and climbed its own tree. Measured over a day, 22061 of one body's 25200 frames were spent standing on leaves 12 m above the valley floor, going up 105 m and back down 114 m, riding the crowns along the ridge. The step test now asks the same question the altitude does — the surface it would stand on, and the room over it all the way up through the body's own span: a metre is a step, rock anywhere over that surface is a wall, and foliage over the head is neither (what is not a wall to a body on foot is read off the material table, and read out in the paragraph above about the body not being a point). And nothing but the ground holds a body up: the height used to be lerped onto whatever the column said, which is an elevator that runs fastest at the moment it starts. Walked off a cliff where the ground falls 17 m within one metre, a body came down 6.8 m in the first 0.1 s (81 m/s), eased the rest of the way to a stop, and finished standing on a tree 5 m above the floor, where it stopped moving. A body on foot now obeys the rule the player's own body and a hopping frog already obeyed — the same gravity, the same terminal speed, the same squash on arrival: the same cliff took 1.17 s, the speed rising from nothing to 26.8 m/s, and the body walked on.
**A body in water swims to its depth, and a body climbing a step climbs at a rate.** A frog and a body on foot are now told apart by whether the data gives the frog a `hop`, not by whether the bodies obey gravity: one routine in `src/entities.js` answers feet, water and the ground for every body. It had to be one routine, because the frog's copy had no bound on what it would do to a height. A frog in water used to be pulled toward just under the surface by a fraction of the distance left each frame, which is the same elevator: dropped on the lakebed 4.7 m down, it came up 0.47 m in its very first frame -- 28 m/s, faster than a falling body. It now closes that depth at the speed its own record swims at (`swimSpeed`: 4.6 m/s for a frog), which is the speed its legs are already moving at, and 9 m of lake takes 2.7 s. The same gap on land: the step test lets a body into a column whose surface is above its feet -- a frog standing on the floor of a lake two blocks deeper than the shelf next door has its feet 1.6 m under that shelf -- and the frog's copy answered by snapping it onto the surface, 1.5 m in one frame (90 m/s). A step is now the step the body's own record gives it. Over a day of the valley, the worst height change any one frame carried by any body went from 1.6 m to 0.28 m.
**A lift is worth keeping only when the body ends up somewhere its own feet could not reach.** A body on foot walked into a riser is lifted by as much as its own `step` allows and the move is tried again from there. The lift used to be tested at the place the body already stands -- where of course there is room -- and the move was then retried with the speed the collision had already zeroed along the axis it had to stop, so the body never moved and the lift was kept anyway: walked at a bank two metres tall on one flat shelf, a human stood on the ground in **34%** of the frames and its height swung **0.124 m** within a second, and even with nothing built in front of it it was on the ground in only **89%** with the same swing -- that is the wobble at the water's edge. Now the retry is handed the move the body asked for, and a lift that ends where the body already was is thrown away: the body goes back down to its feet and stands there. After, the human stands on the ground in every frame with a swing of **0**, and it gains **0** against a step taller than its own legs -- its record says its legs reach **0.55 m**, and the bank stands a metre up. The frog at that same bank is off the ground **77%** of the time, because hopping is what its own record says it does on land, and its feet come back down, which the step asks of every body. A step is not a wall and a wall is not a step; the bound is the body's own `step`, and which of the two it was is answered by where the body ends up, not by the lift.

**A body at rest is at rest, and so is the frame it is in.** The surface a body stops on is the face its feet crossed, and the answer used to be "the higher of that face and where the feet already were" -- which for anything standing is the feet themselves. So a body on flat ground was put back half a millimetre above where it had been at the start of the frame: gravity pulled it down 6.4 mm, the snap lifted it back, and that is a 6 mm sawtooth about five times a second that never stops. The lens sits on the feet, so the whole picture rides it, and it rides it hardest close to the lens -- the blocks near you shake and the ones past 15 m do not. Measured at a wall 2.5 m off with nobody holding a key: **0.74%** of the pixels within 2 m of the lens changed from one frame to the next (mean change 106 of 765), **0.52%** at 5-15 m, **nothing** past 15 m, and the body's own height swung **6.4 mm** and reversed direction **53 times** in five seconds. After: the body does not move at all, it rests 0.5 mm off the face it stands on, and **no pixel** of the frame changes between two frames. A fall is untouched -- for a falling body the face was always below the feet -- and it still arrives at 11.5 m/s and stops *on* the surface rather than 3 cm above it. The cells above the feet are a ceiling and are no longer folded into the same maximum: that maximum could lift a body a whole body-height when its head was in a wall.

**The water holds up a body that breathes water.** A body in the lake used to carry the same fixed downward pull whatever it was made of, so a frog let go of in mid-water sank at a terminal 0.89 m/s, landed on the lakebed 3 m below, was reported standing on the bottom, and stopped there: a water animal that stopped moving sank. The valley's own frogs have always done the opposite -- the creature step holds them just under the surface and never through the bottom -- so the body the player wears was the only water-breathing body in the valley that went down. One rule and one number now: the water holds such a body **at the depth its own record says a body of that kind hangs at, measured to the crown of it** -- the top face of its topmost water block, which is `waterTop + 1`, not the index of that block, and the level is that surface less the height of the body itself less that depth, which is `hover` on the creature row and **0.3 m** for anything that does not state one. The frog's row states **1.6 m**, and that is a decision about the lake rather than about the frog: a body that floats with its back at the surface and its eyes above it is an animal standing in a puddle, not one swimming in a lake, and from the bank it is a cork with four legs under it -- clamped by the bottom, and the water holds it there whether or not its feet happen to be on something, which is what brings a frog that has dived to the lakebed back up the moment the dive key comes off. The level used to be taken off the index, and that put every body the water holds a full block under the surface: a frog half a metre tall floated with its head under the water, and the wild frogs of Mirrorlake hung 1.3 m down where they hang 0.8 m down. The **reference point** is the whole of the matter. That 0.3 m used to be measured to the feet, and a body half a metre tall therefore hung with its head 20 cm out of the water: seen from the bank it was a cork with four legs under it, and the eyes of the body you wear -- 0.42 m up from its own feet -- sat 12 cm *above* the surface, so the animal built for the lake looked at the shore and never once into it. Measured to the crown, the number a row states is what its own sentence always said it was -- how far a floating back sits under the surface -- and the whole body hangs in: at the depth the frog's row states, feet **2.1 m** down, back **1.6 m** down, eyes **1.68 m** down, looking into the lake rather than at the shore. The level is the body's own height plus the depth its row states, so a bigger water animal hangs deeper by exactly its own size, and the only number a species carries is the one sitting on its own row. The dive itself is untouched -- a body that asks to go down swims down, and the lakebed is still reachable: 4 s of dive key still arrives at 8.001 against a bed of 8. ## 4. Codex text (implemented in game)

One entry per species, always written as "verifiable fact + unverifiable rumour":

- **Mirrorlake Frog**: after rain the whole shore calls at once, loud enough to drown out the bell tower. They do not drink — they drink through their skin.
- **Lanternfish**: the water of Mirrorlake. The lanternfish swim the open water of the lake; the sunken tower is only what they steer round now and then — from the surface at night, the tower still glows. They neither chase you nor run from you; swim through them and they treat you as water, which your body is not. Nobody knows why they are lit.

## 5. Plants (not creatures, and not powers)

| id | 中文 | English | Rarity | Effect |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| `cellarseed` | 窖树种 | Cellar Stone | rare | Grows into a **cellar tree**: a 24-slot field warehouse, decay 1/3 |

The valley used to carry five more of these, and four of them were powers you ate — a light for five minutes, a jump, a quietness, a skin against the sting — while the fifth was a fixed point of light. All five are cut, and with them the buff that carried them: a power you eat is not a law the valley runs on, it is a spell with a duration, and the valley keeps its spells out of its body. What a plant may do now is the one thing the world lets it do: **grow**. Cellar stones are not farmed: they stand where the world puts them, and it puts them in exactly two places — one under the oldest tree of the Old Orchard, one near the town — so the stone a forager carries is rare, it costs 4 of the 24 slots, and the decision a trip makes is which four of its slots it is.

## 6. Identity and naming (multiplayer readiness)

**The protagonist's name is not part of the setting.** The source of truth is `IDENTITY` in `game/data/player.js`.

Identity is split into three layers, and only the first survives a move to multiple players:

| Layer | Field | Decided by | Mutable | Unique | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| id | `id` | the system | no | yes | prefix `p-`, random idBytes 6 characters. This is all the world stores: cellar-tree ownership, the lake's ledger |
| epithet | `epithet` | the world (by your deeds) | it changes | not guaranteed | all fiction text uses only this. Default "the fruit counter", upgraded as you do things |
| name | `name` | the player (press N) | yes | no | max nameMax 16 characters. Leaving it empty is perfectly fine |

### Why the worldview demands this anyway

The Grey Veil takes names first — it makes you forget *why you meant to come back*, and a name is the anchor of coming back. So every institution in the valley runs without names: the cellar keeps its books by shape and deed, the volunteer patrol recognises people by epithet, a frog recognises a shape and nothing else. **"Not using names" is a corollary of the worldview, not a concession to multiplayer.**

### The epithet table (`IDENTITY.epithets`)

| Trigger | Epithet | 中文 |
|---|---|---|
| default | the fruit counter | 数果子的 |
| cellar | the cellar-keeper | 存果人 |
| lake | the one who swam Mirrorlake | 泅过镜湖的人 |
| all deeds | the one the valley remembers | 谷地记得的人 |

A later one overrides an earlier one; when it changes the game announces it once ("the valley has started calling you …").

### Writing rules (enforced by a test)

1. **No line of fiction may interpolate the player's name.** Use the epithet, or the second person. `docs-sync.js` fails when a fixed protagonist name appears in the documents.
2. The name appears only in UI (top-left HUD, the codex "you" block) — never in NPC lines, codex entries or the log.
3. An epithet is **the world's verdict**, so it may be plain or even slighting (the default "the fruit counter" is) — renaming yourself does not change what the valley thinks of you.

### Multiplayer: already in place / still missing

**In place**: `id` is saved, cellar trees carry an `owner`, epithets are event-driven, and name and identity are decoupled.

**Still missing**: an identity service (the `id` issued by a server), storage ownership and sharing rights (the main cellar is currently communal), NPCs remembering dialogue per id, and the resolution of attitude-matrix conflicts such as "two players walk into Mirrorlake as frogs at the same time".
